INTRODUCTION
Local governments have become instrumental leaders
in the clean energy transition. Nearly 200 US cities
and counties and eight states have committed to 100%
clean energy to date, covering approximately one in
three Americans.1 Large-scale off-site renewable power
purchase agreements (PPAs) have been the primary
procurement method used to make progress on these
goals, covering nearly 90% of the 9.2 gigawatts (GW)
that local governments have procured since 2015.2 Offsite PPAs will likely continue to be a key strategy given
their large scale and ability to act as a financial hedge.
As with any energy procurement, off-site PPAs also
come with risks. The wholesale market nature of
off-site PPAs produces intricacies and financial risks
that are not present in other renewable energy
procurement strategies. For local governments without
prior experience operating in wholesale markets,
these may be new and unfamiliar.

This paper aims to help local governments (hereafter
referred to as “cities” for simplicity) understand and
mitigate key risks by explaining what they are and the
strategies to mitigate them, as shown in Exhibit 1.

• The paper describes six specific risks: price, basis,

non-energy market, shape, operational, and volume.
While legal and accounting risks are also relevant
to PPAs, they are outside of the scope of this paper.
However, they are outlined in the American Cities
Climate Challenge Renewables Accelerator’s Virtual
Power Purchase Agreement Legal Considerations
report. 3

• The paper then summarizes nine mitigation

strategies for virtual PPAs and physical PPAs.
Some of these strategies are widely used (e.g.,
floors and collars, hub-settled contracts, good
faith renegotiation, and production guarantees)
while others are emerging in the market today (e.g.,
fixed-volume price swaps, retailer-firmed power,
settlement guarantee agreements, volume-firming
agreements, and proxy generation).

Portions of this content have been adapted from RMI’s
A Corporate Purchaser’s Guide to Risk Mitigation and
the Renewable Energy Buyers Alliance’s web-based
Risk Allocation Primer.4

VIRTUAL AND PHYSICAL PPAS
Off-site renewable energy projects are front-of-themeter systems tied into the grid at the transmission
or distribution level. PPAs are the preferred contract
mechanism for these systems in which a buyer agrees
to purchase the energy produced by the project
over a set period of time at a predetermined price
per unit of energy, such as a megawatt-hour (MWh).
Since these agreements provide the developer with
the revenue certainty required to finance the project,
the buyer (i.e., a city) can claim that its purchase
is instrumental in adding new renewable energy
generation to the grid.
There are two primary types of off-site PPAs: virtual
PPAs (VPPAs) and physical PPAs (PPPAs).

VIRTUAL POWER PURCHASE
AGREEMENTS
A VPPA is a purely financial contract where, for each
renewable MWh, the buyer pays the project owner
a fixed $/MWh rate and receives the variable hourly
market $/MWh rate in return, as shown in Exhibit 2.
In most cases, the buyer also takes ownership of
the renewable energy certificates (RECs), which are
created alongside every MWh of renewable energy
generated, to claim the environmental attributes. The
project owner sells the renewable electricity into the
local wholesale electricity market and passes that
revenue onto the buyer.

8 | RMI

Since the buyer is effectively receiving the financial
difference between the PPA price and wholesale
market price for each MWh, these deals are also known
as “contracts for differences,” “fixed-for-floating swaps,”
or “financial PPAs.” Unlike a PPPA, a VPPA is separate
from, and does not impact, the buyer’s power supply
agreements with its local utility or retail provider (unless
the VPPA is contracted through the utility).
Corporations pioneered the use of VPPAs to support
large-scale renewable energy projects and meet their
renewable energy goals. Since VPPAs do not involve
physical delivery of the energy, corporations have
used them to procure renewable energy to offset
the energy usage at sites distributed across multiple
geographies, regardless of whether they are located
in deregulated or regulated energy markets. A VPPA’s
purely financial nature has also enabled companies
to quickly procure renewable energy at scale without
having to create an internal energy management team
to deal with physical energy challenges inherent with
PPPAs. This has allowed companies such as Fifth Third
Bank to meet 100% renewable energy goals through a
single VPPA.5
Cities are also starting to consider VPPAs due to
the structure’s geographic and regulatory flexibility.
Cities located outside a retail choice market that are
not served by a municipal utility or community choice
aggregation (CCA) cannot utilize a PPPA without going
through their utility due to regulatory restrictions.
However, they can still sign a VPPA to support projects
located in wholesale markets. For example, Arlington
County, Virginia, procured 38 megawatts (MW) of solar
through a VPPA with its utility in 2020.6

PHYSICAL POWER PURCHASE
AGREEMENTS
In a PPPA, the buyer (i.e., a city) pays the project owner
a fixed $/MWh rate for each renewable MWh generated
and delivered to a predetermined delivery point within
the local electricity market, as shown in Exhibit 3. In this
structure, the buyer takes legal title to the electricity
and then purchases any additional needed electricity
from its utility (or retailer) in the usual manner.

When they have the option to choose between a
VPPA and a PPPA, most cities have opted to sign
PPPAs to date. For example, the City of Cincinnati
considered both deal structures for its 100 MW solar
PPA for municipal and community load, but ultimately
signed a physical deal.7 Load-serving branches of
local governments, such as municipal utilities and
CCA programs, generally want to physically purchase
renewable energy to supply their customers.

Physical transactions are also easier to conceptualize
and explain. And since they must be executed with a
project in the same independent system operator (ISO) or
region as the city, they have the clear potential to produce
local economic, workforce, and health benefits for their
residents (although VPPAs can also provide these local
benefits if located within the city’s region. Additionally,
cities have preferred PPPAs due to the lower perceived
risk in physical deals as compared with VPPAs. The VPPA
structure more explicitly exposes a city to wholesale
market prices, which can be challenging to cities that are
used to paying flat utility or retail rates for their energy.
While VPPAs and PPPAs are fundamentally different
contract structures, it is also important to note that they
can result in similar financial outcomes in cases where the

city is already purchasing wholesale power. For example,
consider the following hypothetical situation: a city enters
into a PPA for a 25 MW solar project that generates
50,000 MWh per year, and future weighted wholesale
prices in the city’s electricity market are, on average, $2/
MWh higher than its PPA rate over the 10-year contract.
If the city entered into a PPPA, it would have saved $1
million on its wholesale market purchases. Similarly, if the
city instead entered into a VPPA with that same project, it
would have received $1 million in revenue (separate from
its business-as-usual electricity purchases). However, if
the VPPA is signed with a project in a different market not
correlated to the city’s load zone prices, this relationship
will no longer hold.i

RISKS
Any strategy to purchase electricity will come with
a mixture of benefits, costs, and risks. Thus, when
considering off-site PPA risks, they should not be
considered in a vacuum but weighed against the
risks of alternative strategies (e.g., purchasing
unbundled RECs at a cost premium without adding
new renewables to the grid) or simply maintaining
the status quo (e.g., exposing the city to the potential
of future electricity price variability and/or carbon
pricing impacts).

This section explores basis, price, non-energy market,
shape, volume, and operational risk. While basis, price,
and non-energy market risks stem from uncertainty
in the variable wholesale electricity market, shape,
volume, and operational risks stem from uncertainty in
the renewable energy generator’s production. Exhibit
4 lists the degree to which buyers may be exposed to
each of these risks in “reference” VPPAs and PPPAs
which do not include potential mitigation strategies.

Off-site PPAs provide cities with a powerful tool to
purchase renewable energy at a large scale, but they
also bring costs or risks which should be understood
and mitigated where necessary. Understanding these
potential risks is the first step in evaluating the costs
and benefits of different risk mitigation strategies (see
Risk Mitigation Strategies section).

PRICE RISK
What It Is
For VPPAs and PPPAs, this is the risk associated
with the movement and volatility of wholesale
electricity prices.

How It Affects Cities
If market prices are above the PPA price, cities with
PPAs can benefit by paying below-market rates for
each MWh. However, if wholesale prices fall below
the PPA price:

• Cities with VPPAs will pay a premium to the project
•

owner for the difference between the VPPA and
market price for each MWh generated.
Cities with PPPAs will pay more for each MWh
generated than they would have if they had
purchased from the wholesale market.

Further Detail
Price risk stems from hourly and long-term fluctuations
in wholesale prices arising from shifts in supply and
demand, fuel prices, and transmission constraints. For
example, an unexpected surge in wind generation due
to a weather front might cause regional market prices to
decrease. If the market prices fall below the PPA price,
cities will be forced to pay above-market rates for the
generated electricity. While the fundamental economics
are similar, this above-market payment impacts cities
differently depending on whether they have signed a
VPPA or a PPPA.
For VPPAs, price risk impacts the revenue received
from or loss paid to the renewable energy asset’s
owner. Exhibit 5 illustrates that when the wholesale
price is higher than the VPPA price, the city makes
money (i.e., the area shaded in green). Conversely,

12 | RMI

when the market price is lower than the VPPA price,
the city loses money (area shaded in red). Over time, if
the cumulative area shaded in green is more than the
cumulative area shaded in red, the deal is profitable
(and vice versa for a loss). In a VPPA, these fluctuations
can also cause day-to-day, month-to-month, and
year-to-year shifts in the profits and losses generated
by the transaction, which should be considered when
structuring the transaction and setting annual budgets.
From an economic payoff perspective, price risk impacts
a PPPA in much the same manner. While the cost of the
energy in a PPPA is fixed, the rise or fall of energy market
prices will dictate whether signing a PPPA saves or loses
money as compared with purchasing energy from the
market or via a retail provider. However, for PPPAs this
economic profit or loss represents an opportunity cost
rather than actual payments the buyer will have to make,
so the price risk volatility does not directly impact the
city’s budget or operations.
Price risk is often the most critical issue to buyers, as
wholesale power prices dictate the deal’s profitability
yet are volatile over time (see Exhibit 6 for annual
average prices in the PJM Interconnection region) and
difficult to predict many years into the future. Since
most PPAs last for 10–20 years, cities should evaluate a
deal’s potential economic upside and downside using
a variety of future pricing scenarios, and not solely rely
on forecasts provided by project developers. Tools like
the American Cities Climate Challenge Renewables
Accelerator’s Solar and Wind Off-Site PPA Economic
Calculator can be useful for this.8

BASIS RISK
What It Is
This is the risk, primarily in VPPAs, that the relationship
between the market price at the renewable generator’s
location and the price the city pays for its electricity
may change due to locational price differences.

How It Affects Cities
Basis risk can reduce a city’s ability to use PPAs to
hedge against future electricity price changes.

Further Detail
One potential benefit of signing a PPA is hedging
against future increases in energy prices. While
cities that sign PPPAs clearly protect themselves
from escalating prices by locking in a set price for
the length of the contract, VPPAs can also provide a
similar protection. For example, if energy prices rise
uniformly by $5, a city that has signed a VPPA will both
pay $5 more for the energy to power its facilities and
receive $5 more from its VPPA revenues.
This type of hedge is only applicable to cities that
already purchase energy from their local wholesale
market since retail prices do not reflect day-to-day or
hour-to-hour variability of wholesale prices. Moreover,
the effectiveness of this hedge can be eroded if the
relationship between prices at different locations in
the market changes over time.
PPAs have three relevant price settlement points: the
node (the generator’s interconnection point price), the
hub (the simple average of node prices within the hub’s
geographic region), and the buyer’s load zone (the loadweighted average of node prices within the load zone).
Basis risk exists between the price at the generator’s
location (i.e., the generation node) and the price the city
pays for its own consumption at its load zone.

14 | RMI

Consider an example in which a city in Virginia signs
a VPPA with a wind farm in West Texas. The wind farm
delivers its power to Node 1 in the ERCOT West Hub
within the Texas power market, as shown in Exhibit 7,
and in return receives the nodal market price. The nodal
prices from Nodes 1–6 are then averaged to generate
the ERCOT West Hub prices.
But while the VPPA revenues are linked to ERCOT
West Node 1, the buyer’s energy expenses are linked
to the PJM Dominion Load Zone prices in Virginia.
Exhibit 8 indicates that ERCOT West Node 1 and
PJM Dominion Load Zone average monthly prices
are well correlated during most months, but in April,
September, and October the higher prices the city
experiences in Virginia are not fully offset by the prices
at ERCOT West Node 1 that remain low or decrease.
This change in the relationship between prices in
Texas and Virginia is an example of basis risk eroding
the VPPA’s financial hedge.
Basis risk is typically less relevant to PPPAs, as the
power is usually generated within or close to the city’s
load zone or local hub. As a result, the prices at the
point of generation and the point of consumption will
likely be very similar. While project owners usually
absorb any differences in these prices in a PPPA,
that is not always the case. For example, the City of
Houston agreed to pay the basis difference between
its load zone and the project’s node in West Texas in
its 50 MW PPA signed in 2017.

NON-ENERGY MARKET RISK
What It Is
This is the risk associated with unforeseen, nonenergy market changes occurring after a VPPA or
PPPA is signed.

How It Affects Cities
Non-energy market changes could impact the
financial performance of the PPA for the city or
the project owner, which might trigger requests to
renegotiate key terms.

Further Detail
While price risk involves the wholesale energy market,
non-energy market risk encompasses risks related to
other potential revenue streams. Renewable energy
projects could be impacted by a range of non-energy
market changes, such as retroactive changes to federal
incentives, the creation of new markets, or adjustments
to policies relating to environmental attributes.
However, this paper focuses on two common nonenergy markets: capacity markets and REC markets
created by renewable portfolio standards (RPS).

In 2019, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission
issued its Minimum Offer Price Rule that ordered PJM
to impose a price floor for nearly all “state-subsidized”
resources that participate in its capacity auctions. This
serves as an example of how new market regulations
can impact the value of wind or solar plants, as this
ruling may significantly reduce the capacity market
revenues for some renewable energy projects.
Furthermore, if the project is located in a state with
a high RPS, the future value of its RECs could have a
large impact on the project’s overall revenue. These
markets can shift dramatically, making their future
value hard to predict. For example, Exhibit 9 illustrates
how Ohio’s REC prices declined compared to prices
in other PJM states when the state reduced its RPS
ambitions in 2014. Cities that choose to sell their
project’s RECs and buy low-cost national RECs in
hopes of increasing the project’s financial outlook are
especially susceptible to this market risk.

Capacity markets aim to provide grid reliability by
paying power producers, on a $/MW basis, for the
capacity they can supply the grid during a specified
future timeframe. For cities entering into a PPA for a
project in an ISO that operates a capacity market,ii this
capacity revenue is either secured by the city, or, more
frequently, by the project owner and indirectly passed
on to the city through a lower PPA price. Thus, cities
that directly secure this revenue are more susceptible
to the risk of capacity market changes.

SHAPE RISK
What It Is
For VPPAs and PPPAs, the source of shape risk
is the uncertainty regarding the timing of the
renewable generation.

How It Affects Cities
Shape risk impacts the financial performance and
hedge value of VPPAs and PPPAs differently.

• For a VPPA, shape risk coupled with price risk
•

creates uncertainty regarding the revenues the
buyer receives.
For a PPPA, shape risk requires the buyer to purchase
or sell energy to manage over- or underproduction,
while also impacting the deal’s opportunity cost.

Further Detail
The intersection of uncertain generation with
uncertain market prices increases the uncertainty
around the project’s revenue with a VPPA and avoided
costs with a PPPA.
For a VPPA, the uncertain generation shape impacts
wholesale revenues received at the project’s settlement
point. Exhibit 10 illustrates how the generation profile
from a solar plant and the corresponding market prices
impact the VPPA’s revenue.

18 | RMI

For a PPPA, uncertain generation impacts the avoided
cost from not purchasing the energy at the market’s
load zone price. Additionally, in a PPPA, when the
renewable generator produces more power than
the city needs, the city is exposed to the generator’s
node price since it must sell the excess power into the
wholesale market.
When evaluating the financial impacts between
pursuing a VPPA and PPPA for a project, the impacts
on a PPPA’s avoided costs are equivalent to those on a
VPPA’s revenue if the city is purchasing energy on the
wholesale market.
Shape risk also impacts a PPA’s hedge effectiveness.
In a PPPA, over- or underproduction from the
project would typically force the city to transact in
the wholesale market, reducing its price certainty.
Meanwhile, in a VPPA, if generation and consumption
are not coincident (and they rarely are), changing
wholesale energy prices throughout the day may lead
to the VPPA revenues not fully offsetting the city’s
energy costs.

OPERATIONAL RISK
What It Is
This is the risk associated with a renewable
generator’s performance being compromised due to
inadequate or failed processes, people, equipment, or
systems, or from external events.

How It Affects Cities
Decreased generation could lead to decreased profit
(or loss) and RECs for the city.

Further Detail
Operational risk is, for the purposes of this paper,
uncertainty in renewable generator performance due to
factors other than weather. Project underperformance
can occur due to important equipment being taken offline for maintenance or degrading due to wear and tear,
operational errors, force majeure events, or intentional
curtailment of generation.
System operators may require generators to
curtail, or limit, their generation when suppliers are
unexpectedly providing more power than the grid
demands at a system-wide level. System operators
may also order curtailments at a local level if the
transmission lines have insufficient capacity to carry
the renewable electricity to where it is needed in
the system. Exhibit 11 illustrates how transmission
constraints can vary over time; transmission
upgrades in West Texas allowed ERCOT to decrease
curtailment from 2012 to 2014 despite increasing
wind penetration.

20 | RMI

Market averages, however, may differ from project
specific curtailment. For example, the average
curtailment within ERCOT was 2.5% in 2018, but four
wind projects totaling nearly 600 MW experienced
curtailment of 18%–25%.9 Project owners may also
curtail their plants intentionally to avoid financial losses
for themselves and/or a buyer if market prices fall
below a certain threshold (e.g., $0/MWh).
Another type of operational risk is a force majeure
event: an unforeseen event causing disruption to the
generator, such as a natural disaster. These types
of events could damage the asset and reduce its
generation potential.
As with volume risk, decreased output will impact the
project’s economic performance and decrease REC
production for both VPPAs and PPPAs, which can
impact a city’s ability to meet its sustainability goals.

VOLUME RISK
What It Is
This is the risk of uncertainty in the overall amount of
renewable energy generation.

How It Affects Cities
For both VPPAs and PPPAs, varying weather patterns
create uncertainty regarding the amount of renewable
generation, which impacts the project’s value and
number of RECs created. In PPPAs, this also creates
uncertainty regarding the amount of additional
balancing energy required.

Further Detail
While the uncertainty in the amount of renewable
energy generated associated with volume risk
could be due to various factors, this paper defines
volume risk as weather-driven to differentiate it from
operational risk. Furthermore, while the timing of
renewable generation inherent in shape risk can
also be weather-driven, volume risk is distinctly
associated with the uncertainty in the overall amount
of renewable generation.
Since the value of a VPPA to a city in any given
period is the product of the energy generated and
the difference between the market and VPPA prices,
any variability in the former causes uncertainty to the
project’s value. This could lead to cash flows being
higher or lower than expected, exposing the city to
upside and downside risk.

opportunity cost of the deal, translating to uncertainty

utility or retailer.

FLOORS AND COLLARS
What It Is
Floors and collars are agreements within the VPPA
contract between the city and project developer that
reduce the city’s exposure to prices below and/or
above a certain threshold. A floor is a minimum limit
for the market price, and thus revenue, that a city can
receive from the sale of the electricity. A collar consists
of minimum and maximum limits within which a city can
receive market prices. These structures are not typically
used in PPPAs since the buyer in physical contracts is
not directly exposed to floating market prices.
As an example, consider a contract that has a collar
with a maximum limit of $30/MWh and a minimum limit
of $10/MWh. If the market price falls to $8/MWh, the
city will still receive $10/MWh in revenue with the extra
$2/MWh being supplemented by the project owner.
If, however, the price rises to $34/MWh, the city will
receive only $30/MWh and the additional $4/MWh
would pass on to the project owner.
Negative pricing protections have become a fairly
common form of floor protection in VPPAs. These are
essentially $0 floors which protect the buyer from

HUB OR LOAD ZONE SETTLEMENT
What It Is
In a VPPA, a city may choose to settle at the hub to
receive the generator’s hub pricing (instead of node
pricing) as its floating market price. The project owner
will still sell the project’s power at the node, meaning
that the owner will have to accept the basis risk
between the project’s node and its hub. Similarly, with
a PPPA, the city may choose to take delivery at its
load zone, in which case the project owner manages
differences between the project and load zone prices.

• Basis risk: Compared with nodal prices, hub or load

zone prices should generally be less volatile and
more correlated with the prices that a city pays to its
energy supplier for delivered electricity. To the extent
that this is true, settling at a hub or load zone will
reduce the basis risk to which the city is exposed.

• Price risk: Hub and load zone prices are, on average,
less volatile than node prices. Thus, settling at
the hub typically reduces the volatility in market
prices the city receives from a VPPA and, therefore,
reduces price risk.

Which City Risks Are Mitigated
Settling at the hub or load zone primarily reduces
basis risk while slightly reducing price risk for VPPAs,
as shown in Exhibit 17.

Where It Has Been Done Before
The cities of Philadelphia and Cincinnati took
electricity delivery within their load zone in their
PPPAs signed in 2019.

GOOD FAITH RENEGOTIATION
What It Is

Which City Risks Are Mitigated

A “good faith renegotiation” clause in a VPPA or PPPA
states that if an impactful regulatory change occurs, the
city and project owner are required to meet and attempt
in good faith to negotiate amendments that as far as
possible restore to both parties the original balance of
burdens and benefits. The rationale is that neither party
knows what changes might occur, and neither party can
control them, so the two parties should share this risk
and renegotiate terms as needed.

A good faith renegotiation clause in a VPPA or PPPA
can reduce non-energy market risk and/or price risk,
as shown in Exhibit 18.

These clauses can incorporate changes to both energy
and non-energy markets to mitigate the risk that the
city and/or project owner would be financially impacted.
Financial thresholds may be specified to determine
when these negotiations should be pursued.
PPAs can also include backstop clauses that specify
a PPA rate change corresponding to specific market
rule changes if parties cannot in good faith agree
to a reasonable amendment (e.g., if an RPS change
impacts average annual project REC prices by at least
+/-75% and parties cannot agree to a price amendment
within one year from the rule change date, the new
PPA rate will change by +/- $2/MWh). Backstop clauses
are most suitable for non-energy (e.g., capacity)
market rule changes.

• Non-energy market risk: Including non-energy

•

market changes within the good faith negotiation
clause can reduce the risk that future regulatory
changes will disproportionately impact the city
and/or project owner. Parties may also want
to include renegotiation triggers in the case of
unexpected upside (e.g., the state strengthens its
RPS and REC prices significantly increase).
Price risk: If this clause incorporates energy market
changes, this strategy may help protect the city
from systemic electricity market price changes that
arise from fundamental market shifts over the life
of the contract (e.g., changes to ISO policies or
pricing methodologies).

Where It Has Been Done Before
In Arlington County’s VPPA, the good faith
renegotiation clause focused on changes to the
utility program that would impose additional costs
on the seller above a specified threshold. The City of
Cincinnati’s PPPA included a good faith renegotiation
clause to address capacity market changes. Finally,
the City of Philadelphia’s PPPA included capacity
market changes, market price availability changes,
and a backstop $/MWh PPA price reduction related to
capacity market changes if the parties could not agree
upon an alternative.

A city can sign a PPPA contract with a retail electricity

Retailer-firmed power eliminates or reduces shape,

In the United States, 17 states and the District of

have the option to arrange for a REP to incorporate
a PPPA into their retail service or ask the retailer to

hourly generation, as well as any long-term over- or
underproduction, to ensure that the city receives the
power it needs at any given moment.

could either stay with the retail provider or the city
could retain the PPPA. As the retail provider would be
managing a variety of risks, the city would have to pay a
premium above a PPPA price for these services.

VOLUME-FIRMING AGREEMENT
What It Is
A volume-firming agreement (VFA) is a financial
transaction added onto VPPAs to eliminate shape and
volume risk. It is also designed to improve a VPPA’s
hedge effectiveness for cities purchasing wholesale
power with flat or otherwise consistent and predicable
electricity demand (e.g., a water treatment plant).
Exhibit 22 shows how, in a normal VPPA, the timing of
renewable energy generation does not match the city’s
consistent load. This disconnect undermines the VPPA’s
effectiveness as a hedge against its unpredictable
business-as-usual (BAU) hourly electricity costs (i.e., a
VPPA is a perfect hedge that results in fixed costs only
during the hours when the renewable energy project
generates exactly the same amount of electricity that
the city physically consumes). VFAs directly resolve
this discrepancy by allowing cities to exchange their
VPPA revenues for what would have been earned if
the project had produced energy at fixed volumes
throughout the course of a day (i.e., the financial
equivalent of firmed power).
As a result, a VFA transfers the financial risks of overor underproduction by a renewable energy project to
an insurer, enabling a city to source renewable energy
and lock in its electricity consumption costs at the
same time (see Exhibit 22). Since VFAs involve a third
counterparty and transfer a significant amount of risk
away from the city, they will involve transaction costs.

mitigate basis risk unless the VFA, VPPA, and load zone settlements are all settled at the same price point.

PRODUCTION GUARANTEES
What It Is
Production guarantees include requirements for when
the developer will commence production, how much
will be produced, or when generation will be available
(all subject to the PPA’s force majeure clause).

• Commercial operation date (COD) guarantees

•

•

38 | RMI

require that the plant begin feeding electricity into
the transmission system by a specified date. COD
deadlines are quite common and typically specify
both an expected COD and guaranteed COD as
well as the repercussions for failing to comply. The
period between expected and guaranteed COD
typically ranges from 90 to 360 days.
Availability guarantees require that the plant be
mechanically able to generate for a specified
percentage of the year (e.g., all plant equipment
must be fully operational for 99.5% of the
year). An availability guarantee is, in effect, a
promise that the project owner will perform
necessary maintenance to ensure that the plant is
mechanically able to perform.
Output guarantees require the plant to generate a
minimum amount of electricity over the course of a
year or some other timeframe (e.g., two-year rolling,
weather-adjusted, 90% guarantee of the P50). An
output guarantee is, in effect, a promise by the
project developer that the plant will actually output
some amount of electricity (and therefore RECs) over
a period of time. Output guarantees are possible but
less common in VPPAs.

It is important to consider what rights the city will have
if the project owner fails to deliver on its guarantees.
In particular, the city may want to specify whether
the project owner will be required to purchase
replacement RECs to make up for production shortfalls.
The city may even consider a REC replacement clause
even in situations where the project owner did not fail
to meet its obligations (e.g., a wind project meets its
mechanical availability requirements but still does not
generate the required number of RECs due to poor
weather conditions).

Which City Risks Are Mitigated
Production guarantees specifically address
operational risk, as shown in Exhibit 24, by providing
some level of protection to the city against project
underperformance.

Where It Has Been Done Before
The City of Cincinnati and City of Philadelphia
utilized an output guarantee with shortfall payment
clauses and economic curtailment specifications in
their PPPAs. The cities of Cincinnati and Philadelphia
and Arlington County specified a required COD.

PROXY GENERATION
What It Is
Proxy generation is a weather resource-based
methodology for calculating the hourly volume of
energy that a given renewable energy project would
have generated had it achieved its planned operational
efficiency. A third-party calculation agent typically
determines proxy generation, translating hourly
measured wind or solar resource data into expected
energy generation.xiii

Additionally, these availability projections typically
focus on annual or seasonal limits, which can have little
impact on exposures created at the hourly level (e.g.,
mechanical shutdowns during an extreme weather
event when power prices tend to be very high).
Similar to production guarantees, REC shortfall concerns
can be alleviated by requiring project owners to deliver
the number of RECs that would have been created
according to the proxy generation calculations.

The buyer is then compensated based on the amount
of proxy generation (a modeled value) as opposed to
metered generation (an observed quantity) from the
renewable energy project. This shields the city from a
range of mechanical and operational risks, including
over- or underperformance of the panels or turbines,
plant or substation failures, maintenance downtime, or
curtailment that could decrease production.
For example, if a 100 MW solar project generates
only 75 MW at noon due to a technical fault, but the
calculated proxy generation was 95 MW, the city’s
VPPA revenue or loss will be based on the 95 MW
[(Market $/MWh price – PPA $/MWh price) X 95 MW].
Similarly, if the project generates more than the proxy
generation, the city’s revenue or loss is still based on
the proxy generation.
Proxy generation provides the added benefit of
simplifying VPPA contract terms. VPPAs based on actual
generation typically attempt to mitigate the buyer’s
exposure to mechanical risk and operational decisions
through availability guarantees, production minimums,
maintenance schedule restrictions, and so forth.
These terms are typically complex, time-consuming to
negotiate, and difficult to monitor and enforce.

CONCLUSION
Cities are increasingly looking to large off-site PPAs
to achieve their renewable energy goals due to their
benefits in scale, geographic flexibility, and financial
hedge potential. When utilizing these procurement
structures it is important that they understand their
inherent risks and the available strategies to mitigate
those risks. Virtual PPA and physical PPA contracts
expose buyers to price, basis, non-energy market,
shape, operational, and volume risk, in order of typical
importance to buyers.
For VPPAs, Exhibit 26 illustrates the degree to which
various market strategies can mitigate those risks.

• Price risk can be limited with floors and collars that
•

42 | RMI

set limits on extreme price fluctuations—or mitigated
even further with financial add-ons like a fixed-volume
price swap or settlement guarantee agreement.
Settling at the project’s hub can reduce basis risk
between the project’s market price and city’s load
zone price.

• Non-energy market rule changes can be mitigated
•

•

with good faith renegotiation clauses.
Shape and volume risks caused by uncertainty in the
timing and overall amount of renewable generation,
respectively, can be mitigated with settlement
guarantee agreement or volume-firming agreement
financial add-ons.
Operational risk can be mitigated with production
guarantees and eliminated with proxy generation
by using a calculated production profile based on
weather conditions instead of actual production.

energy to the city’s load zone, cities will more likely

PPPA buyers in retail choice states can partner with

PPPAs typically include production guarantees to

Ultimately, the decision of which type of PPA to
pursue and which risks to mitigate will depend
on each individual city’s particular circumstances
and preferences. While perceived to be more
complex, VPPAs can provide beneficial geographic

44 | RMI

flexibility, which may be of particular value to cities
facing regulatory constraints or unattractive local
market dynamics. PPPAs, meanwhile, provide a
conceptually straightforward mechanism that reduces
a city’s uncertainty regarding its long-term energy
expenditures. Yet both structures will change a city’s
overall energy strategy and can reduce some existing
risks while simultaneously introducing new ones.
The strategies outlined in this paper provide a range
of options for cities to mitigate these new risks, some
of which may come at a cost premium depending on
the degree of risk off-loaded. These risk mitigation
strategies can allow cities to overcome internal
roadblocks and, through PPAs, unlock a range of
financial, sustainability, economic development, health,
and other benefits for their communities.
