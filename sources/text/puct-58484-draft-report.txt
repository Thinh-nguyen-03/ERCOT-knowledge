Current 4CP Methodology
A.

History

This section provides a historical overview concerning relevant legislative action, rulemaking, and
Commission decisions on the use of the ERCOT four coincident peak (4CP) in the Commission's
regulated rate setting practice. For both wholesale transmission rates and retail transmission rates,
the usage of 4CP derives its origins from the transition to competition driven by legislative action

in the 1990s.
4CP in the Development of Wholesale Transmission Rates

Prior to the unbundling and deregulation of the wholesale energy market in the ERCOT region,
wholesale transmission rates were not standardized. In 1982, the Commission recognized the need

for a rule to establish transparent, fair, and reasonable rates for transmission service that avoided
subsidization of wholesale costs by a utility's retail customers. The Commission adopted a rule in
1982 that prescribed two formulas for calculating a facilities charge. Each methodology produced
a facilities charge assessed in dollars per megawatt (MW) for each transaction. For transactions
larger than 25 MW, the formula was based on a transaction's impact on a utility's transmission
system "megawatt-miles". 1 This calculation was a somewhat complex operation that required
transmission line thermal ratings, length of the line in miles, and peak power flow studies for each
transaction. Transactions that were 25 MW or less were assessed a charge by taking the utility's
total annual transmission costs and dividing by system peak demand.2
Preliminary methodologies set out by this rule remained in place with minimal changes until the
1990s. The passage of SB 3733 in 1995 began a near decade-long period that brought about

numerous changes to both the wholesale energy market and the competitive retail market.
Senate Bill (SB) 373 added Public Utility Regulatory Act (PURA) §§ 2.056 and 2.057, directing
the Commission to initiate the process of deregulating the wholesale energy market in the ERCOT
region. The Commission then replaced many existing transmission service rules for consistency
with the new provisions of PURA. New 16 Texas Administrative Code (TAC) § 23.67 required
each transmission service provider (TSP) to "provide wholesale transmission service to other

electric utilities, power marketers, exempt wholesale generators, and qualifying facilities," at both
transmission and distribution voltage.4 Under this commission rule, utilities in the ERCOT region

were also required to begin the process of functionally unbundling costs associated with the
generation, transmission, and distribution rates on customer bills.5 Perhaps most importantly, this
iteration created the first version of the ERCOT Independent System Operator to manage the

The creation of a wholesale energy market meant that a new standardized method for determining
wholesale transmission rates was required. The Commission evaluated several different

methodologies, ranging from various distance-sensitive approaches to the uniform postage stamp
methodology used today. Ultimately, the Commission opted for a two-part rate formula that
reflected the usage of an entirely interconnected grid. This new rate applied to all utilities operating
within the boundaries of the ERCOT region.
The first part of the new rate, an "access fee," collected 70% of the total transmission cost of
service (TCOS) in the ERCOT region and was based on the ERCOT-wide postage stamp rate. The

remaining 30% was an "impact fee" based on the vector-absolute "megawatt-mile" methodology. 6
No interim update mechanism existed to update a utility's TCOS in between comprehensive base
rate proceedings. Recognizing the magnitude of change required by this rule, the Commission also
implemented a phase-in mechanism to mitigate any outsized rate impact and to allow "utilities
who might suffer an adverse transmission cost impact adequate time to adjust to the new
transmission pricing" required by the new rule.7
Determining the access fee portion was relatively simple. Each utility's access fee was calculated
by taking 70% of the total TCOS of all TSPs in the ERCOT region and dividing by the average
ERCOT peak demand over the 4CP intervals to determine a $/4CP-MW rate. The average of the
utility's demand during the ERCOT 4CP intervals was then multiplied by the $/MW rate to
determine the annual access fee portion of the transmission facilities charge. The overall goal of

the postage stamp portion of the annual charge was to provide a simple, transparent, and
predictable rate to promote competition within the wholesale energy market in the ERCOT region.
Calculating the impact fee consistent with the vector-absolute "megawatt-mile" method required
an evaluation of the system "megawatt-mile" impacts related to each individual utility and was
typically performed during a TCOS proceeding. The goal of the impact fee was to encourage some
level of economic efficiency among the usage of the transmission system between generators and
the load served by basing charges in part upon the distance that power had to flow The variance

ofthe vector-absolute "megawatt-mile" calculation was designed to impose a higher cost on energy
consumers who were further away from a generator, and therefore "used" more ofthe transmission
system for consumption of power. Combining the impact fee with the access fee resulted in an
annual facilities charge, which was divided by the twelve months and billed to each utility per 4CP
MW monthly.

require the Commission to price ERCOT wholesale transmission rates based entirely on the
postage stamp methodology. Next, rulemaking proceedings adopted an amended 16 TAC § 25.192

4CP in the Development of Retail Electric Delivery Service Rates

The use of 4CP in the development of retail rates originates from the deregulation and unbundling
requirements of SB 7. Three new entities were created as a result of the functional unbundling of
the vertically integrated investor-owned utilities: (1) a power generating company (PGC), (2) a
retail electric provider (REP), and (3) a transmission and distribution utility (TDU). 10 The new
PGCs and REPs were no longer subject to the traditional tariff regulation performed by the
Commission. The newly created TDUs were still natural monopolies, and Commission rate
regulation and open access oversight remained. The first unbundled cost of service (UCOS)
proceedings were initiated for the affected utilities, and Docket No. 2234411 (the generic issues
docket) was opened to address any generally applicable issues associated with the inaugural TDU
base rate proceedings. Three key decisions made by the Commission in the generic issues docket
created the foundation for the use of 4CP in setting retail transmission rates.
First, the Commission determined that TSPs should bill distribution service providers (DSPs) for
wholesale transmission service. Two methods for TSP cost recovery were presented to the
Commission: (1) TSPs direct billing REPs and (2) TSPs billing DSPs. The Commission chose the
second option for a multitude of reasons. 12 The direct billing methodology would have introduced
a new billing relationship between nearly 40 TSPs and each individual REP, significantly
complicating the billing process for REPs, the costs of which could be reflected in competitive
retail rates. The direct billing methodology also raised concerns from municipally owned utilities

and electric cooperatives regarding the appropriate process undertaken to bill customers in their
service areas, should they choose to opt in to retail competition. In contrast, DSP billing followed
a preexisting, simpler process. As discussed in the wholesale rate section, each DSP's assessment
of wholesale transmission charges was determined by its 4CP demand.

The Commission sought to match rate design with cost causation as much as possible while
acknowledging the practical limitations of metering technology at that time. Therefore, the
Commission adopted a rate design for each class that reflected the best-available metering data for
that class at that time. For customers with an interval data recorder (IDR) meter, this meant that

these transmission charges are allocated to the rate classes. For this reason, the Commission

kW billing determinants were to be used, given that these customers' 4CP data was not available.
Furthermore, for residential and other small secondary customers that did not have demand meters,
transmission charges were to be recovered on a per-kWh basis. This metering-based rate design
approach ensured that rates were as cost-responsive as the available metering technology allowed.
Finally, the Commission determined that it was appropriate for DSPs to pass through wholesale
transmission charges directly to the REPs serving their customers. 14 A provision of § 25.192 now

transmission rate on an interim basis each year. This meant that an IOU DSP, over a four-year

a rule to allow IOU DSPs the ability to pass these changes along to REPs serving their customers

The Commission also approved an unopposed settlement agreement among the parties to the
generic issues docket. The agreement determined that using a rate class's share of the IOU DSP's
4CP demand was the appropriate way to allocate transmission costs among the classes. This
approach established rate class allocation factors and provided uniformity across each IOU DSP,
ensuring the retail transmission rates aligned with wholesale transmission costs to the extent
feasible with the metering technology widely available at the time.

The three Commission decisions, combined with the agreement on allocation factors, provided the
structure for reflecting wholesale transmission charges in retail rates applied to REPs. Within each
initial UCOS proceeding, class allocation factors based on a utility's 4CP demand were
implemented and used to determine the base transmission revenue requirement for each rate class.
The same 4CP-based allocators were used in each TCRF proceeding that followed to determine
the proper assignment of each rate class's share of wholesale transmission costs. An adjustment
was made to the TCRF formula in 2010, allowing IOU DSPs to "true-up" the amounts collected
from the TCRF. The under- or over-collected amounts were then applied to the revenue
requirement established in the next TCRF proceeding. Outside of this formulaic change, the
process of setting retail transmission rates based on 4CP has remained largely unchanged since the
initial UCOS proceedings from 25 years ago.
As a result of this history, the status-quo transmission cost recovery construct means:
(1)

Wholesale transmission cost recovery by a TSP is based on the TSP's rate multiplied
by DSP 4CP demand. The resulting revenues recovered by TSPs increase when 4CP
demand increases and decrease when 4CP demand decreases.

(2)

For IOU DSPs, the wholesale transmission charges paid by that DSP are allocated
among rate classes based upon each rate class's share of that DSP's 4CP demand from
the test year in the DSP's most recent base rate proceeding to establish that class's
revenue requirement.

(3)

For the retail transmission rates IOU DSPs charge to REPs, TCRF rates for each rate

class are designed to recover the class revenue requirements along with true-ups for
past under- or over-collections. TCRF rates for residential and smaller non-residential
customers are per-kWh energy charges. TCRF rates for mid-sized non-residential
customers are NCP demand charges, and TCRF rates for larger non-residential
customers are 4CP demand charges.
B.

Pros and cons list

The existing 4CP methodology for wholesale transmission rate design, retail class cost allocation,

and rate design practices provides the following benefits:
(1)

Wholesale 4CP rate design combined with 4CP class allocation and 4CP retail rates,
sends a conservation and demand response price signal. This price signal incentivizes
non-opt-in-entity (NOIE) DSPs and customers billed on a 4CP basis to respond in a
manner that reduces peak demand on the transmission system during times of
transmission scarcity. This retail rate design component can also help REPs determine
the hedge value of an energy efficiency program for customers in competitive choice

areas.
(2)

Wholesale 4CP rate design allows revenue and rate stability, with 4CP values
fluctuating less than 3% per year on average. TSPs can accurately estimate annual
revenues for the subsequent year shortly after September. Stable billing units support
predictable TSP rate revenue, which in turn benefits ratepayers via lower-thanotherwise financing costs to be recovered through rates.

5

(3)

The transparency and predictability associated with wholesale 4CP rate design enables
DSPs, including NOIE DSPs, which predominately serve residential and small non-

residential customers, to reasonably estimate and plan for their subsequent year's
transmission charges based on their own 4CP demand being determined after
September of each year.
Shortcomings of the existing 4CP methodology include the following:
(1)

The 4CP methodology does not capture transmission scarcity which has recently
occurred during winter season peak periods.

(2)

The use of a single peak from each of the four "summef' months does not adequately

reflect significant scarcity on the transmission system that occurs outside of those four
intervals-notably during solar ramp hours in the morning and evening-and which
may reflect higher scarcity than occurs during the other three summer months that
comprise the 4CP methodology.
(3)

The use of 15-minute intervals to establish the peak period in each month likely

provides too "sharp" of a price signal in too narrow a timeframe, thereby over-valuing
the amount of peak demand reduction or avoidance achieved in relation to the
transmission costs avoided.
(4)

Some types of sophisticated large industrial consumers can reasonably predict 4CP
intervals and rapidly reduce consumption during likely peak hours, thus creating a
mismatch in allocation factors on average over time.

(5)

As more flexible large loads, such as crypto mines and certain types of data centers,
interconnect to the ERCOT transmission system, clear transmission cost assignment
may become increasingly difficult. These flexible large loads require significant
transmission system investment and consume electricity on an order of magnitude
higher than most other consumers. Their flexible operating procedures allow them to
quickly reduce consumption without significantly disrupting their core business
functions. This will become both a feature and a bug of the 4CP methodology.
C.

Impacts to wholesale market

The reduction in demand resulting from customer 4CP price response behavior can introduce
impacts on the wholesale energy market, particularly when such behavior is motivated by the
avoidance of transmission charges rather than by real-time system conditions reflected in energy
prices.
In ERCOT's wholesale energy market, demand reductions are typically expected to occur as a
response to high energy prices, which signal real-time generation scarcity. In contrast, the 4CP
methodology introduces an additional--and often more significant--form of price-responsive

behavior, where customers decrease demand primarily to reduce wholesale transmission charges
rather than to avoid high real-time energy prices. Historically, ERCOT summer system peaks
coincided with periods of high energy prices. However, the increasing market penetration of

renewable generation resources has shifted this relationship so that high prices now tend to have a

6

stronger relationship with peak net load (defined as gross load minus wind and solar generation).
Peak net load typically occurs during the evening solar ramp down.

However, reductions in demand that result from 4CP price signals are intended to align with

transmission scarcity and are not specifically intended to align with periods of high energy prices.
High energy prices provide a market signal indicating generation scarcity, which should ultimately
drive investment and retention of generation resources needed for long-term reliability. That said,

reductions in demand as a response to out-of-market financial incentives impact market outcomes
by suppressing energy prices below the level they would be absent this behavior. If 4CPincentivized demand reductions were to more often occur during these periods when high energy
prices often occur, there would be a greater overall alignment of transmission scarcity with

generation scarcity to create effective wholesale and retail pricing signals.
In summary, 4CP-incentivized demand reductions may impact price formation and observed load
patterns without a corresponding relationship to times of actual generation scarcity. Historically,

when high energy prices tended to coincide with high gross load rather than high net load,
4CP-incentivized demand reductions likely had a more substantial price suppression effect because
those reductions in demand occurred during the very hours when generation was most scarce. As
generation scarcity pricing conditions have become more associated with periods of high net load,
the degree of price suppression resulting from 4CP-incentivized demand reductions may be
diminished relative to prior years.

Overview of Staff's Analyses: Subsection A presents analyses that provide an overview of the
historical data collected, detailing historical trends in customer utilization and cost assessment,
describing the relative magnitudes of coincident peaks throughout different parts of the year, and
investigating the nature of CP-incentivized demand reduction (CPDR). While the ERCOT
provided data gives separate estimates for the aggregate amount of CPDR in competitive choice
areas and in NOIE service areas, the analyses described in this report restrict attention to CPDR in
competitive choice areas to align with other available data.
Subsection B presents the results of a Monte Carlo-based analysis designed to estimate the firstorder impacts of transitioning to alternative cost assessment methodologies. Specifically, the
results provide estimates ofthe impact to the relative share of costs assessed to each customer class
under a pre-defined set of methodologies, and more generally, from modifying the cost assessment

Relative Magnitude of Daily Peaks - This section presents analyses comparing the relative

magnitudes of monthly peaks, measured as a percentage of annual peak demand in the same year.
One rationale for the current 4CP methodology, which only considers the coincident peaks in June,
July, August, and September, is that the ERCOT system peaks in the summer and peak demand is
a significant driver of the need for new transmission investment. As such, the 4CP methodology
both assigns transmission costs based on usage during times when the transmission system is most
utilized and incentivizes demand reduction during these same periods. While the ERCOT system
continues to peak in the summer, recent trends have raised questions about whether this evaluation
window should be expanded to consider other parts of the year as well. Winter peaks have grown
substantially in recent years, even surpassing 90% ofthe annual peak demand value in some years.
Figure 8 depicts, for each month, the magnitude of the monthly peak as a percentage of the annual
peak in the corresponding year. Unsurprisingly, monthly peaks in the summer (June, July, August,

and September) are consistently high, often above 95% ofthe annual peak and almost exclusively
above 90%. While non-summer peaks are generally lower, there are some notable instances of
months with relatively large peaks. The monthly peak during one of the winter months (January,
February, and December) exceeded 90% of the annual peak during three of the five years
examined: February 2021, December 2022, and January 2024.

described in the previous section, collected from ERCOT and TDUs in the ERCOT region, staff
first developed three different statistical approaches (linear regression, spline regression, and
random forest) for estimating CPDR behavior during non-summer months. Each historical year
(2020-2024) was then simulated multiple times under several different alternative cost assessment
methodologies to obtain estimates around the expected outcomes. This analysis provides estimates

Cost Assessment Methodologies - The following list describes the set of cost assessment
methodologies examined in this section of the report. Each methodology described below is based
on a coincident peak formulation, but the method of determining a given CP varies across
mechanisms. In the 4CP (baseline), 7CP, and 12CP cases, a CP is the highest demand interval
within a given month. The remaining mechanisms introduce the concept of floating CPs, such as
floating summer (FS) and floating winter (FW). Floating CPs are determined by comparing daily
peak intervals across time frames longer than a fixed calendar month. If a floating CP window
overlaps with fixed CP months (e.g., 5CP (4CP + FS) introduces a floating summer CP in addition
to the four monthly CPs in the baseline 4CP case), the floating CP is the highest demand interval
that does not occur on the same day as a monthly CP.

•

4CP (baseline) cost assessment is based on demand during each of the monthly CPs in
the summer (June, July, August, and September).

•

5CP (4CP + FW): cost assessment is based on demand during each of the monthly CPs in
the summer and a floating winter CP.

•

5CP (4CP + FS): cost assessment is based on demand during each of the monthly CPs in
the summer and a floating summer CP.

•

6CP ( 4CP + FW + FS) cost assessment is based on demand during each of the monthly
CPs in the summer, a floating winter CP, and a floating summer CP.

•

7CP: cost assessment is based on demand during each of the monthly CPs in the summer
(June, July, August, and September) and each of the monthly CPs in the winter (January,
February, and December).

•

SCP (7CP + FW) cost assessment is based on demand during each of the monthly CPs in
the summer, each of the monthly CPs in the winter, and a floating winter CP.

•

8CP (7CP + FS): cost assessment is based on demand during each of the monthly CPs in
the summer, each of the monthly CPs in the winter, and a floating summer CP.

•

9CP ( 7CP + FW + FS) cost assessment is based on demand during each of the monthly
CPs in the summer, each of the monthly CPs in the winter, a floating winter CP, and a
floating summer CP.

12CP: cost assessment is based on demand during all monthly CPs.

Approaches for Estimating CP-Incentivized Demand Response - Under the existing 4CP

methodology, some large consumers can reduce the amount of transmission charges they
ultimately pay by strategically curtailing demand during each of the four assessed CP intervals.
For the purposes of this report, this type of behavior is referred to as CP-Incentivized Demand
Reduction (CPDR). Data obtained from ERCOT provides estimates of the extent to which this has

17

occurred, on aggregate, during existing CP months (June, July, August, and September). However,
ifthe cost assessment methodology were to change, this type of demand reduction behavior would
likely adjust accordingly. Since no historical CPDR data exists for non-summer months, these
values must be estimated using the information available.
A variety of analytical techniques were employed to impute expected aggregate CPDR values for
other times of the year. Specifically, CPDR estimates are generated using three distinct approaches:

a linear regression model, a quadratic spline regression model, and a random forest machine
learning algorithm. Each of these approaches is described in additional detail in the following
sections. Using multiple methods provides additional insights into the underlying data and

enhances the robustness of the results by reducing reliance on any single approach.

between a dependent variable and one or more independent variables. A linear regression
fits a straight line (or hyperplane, in the case of multiple independent variables) that best
represents the data by minimizing the sum of squared errors (the difference between actual
and predicted values). The resulting linear equation can then be used to estimate the value
of the dependent variable for any combination of independent variables.

Spline Regression: A flexible statistical method designed to capture complex, non-linear
relationships in data. Whereas the linear regression approach described above assumes a
single linear relationship between the dependent and independent variables, spline
regression adapts to localized variation by fitting multiple polynomial functions over
different segments ofthe data. These polynomials are joined together at points called knots
to form a smooth, continuous function that can be used to predict the value ofthe dependent
variable for any combination of values of the independent variables. The flexibility of this
approach improves predictive accuracy in situations where relationships between variables
are non-linear or exhibit localized variation in different segments of the data.

20

The first step in estimating a spline regression model is selecting appropriate
hyperparameters (settings that control how the model learns from the data). This involves

choosing three specific values: the polynomial degree, the number of knots, and the knot
placement strategy. The degree of the polynomial determines the flexibility within each

segment-a higher degree polynomial can capture more non-linearity but risks overfitting
to the existing data. The number of knots determines how many segments the data is

divided into-too few knots will produce a poor fit while too many can make the resulting
equation unnecessarily complex. Two common strategies exist for knot placement strategy:

uniform (knots are evenly spaced across the range of the independent variable) and
quantile-based (knots are placed at specific quantiles ofthe data).
A tuning algorithm that conducts an exhaustive search over every combination of a
specified set of values was used to determine the optimal hyperparameters for this model.
This algorithm evaluated degree two (quadratic) and three (cubic) polynomials, with
anywhere from two to five knots placed either uniformly or at quantile-based locations.
For each combination of hyperparameters, the model was trained on a subset of the data
and validated on another subset to prevent overfitting to the training set. The algorithm
compared the performance of each model to determine the hyperparameters that provide
the best predictive accuracy. The best configuration was degree two (quadratic spline), two
knots, and uniform knot placement. This combination provides enough flexibility to
capture localized variation while remaining relatively simple and avoiding overfitting.
Figure 11 depicts the predictions of the quadratic spline regression with the selected
hyperparameters. The points in the graph represent actual CPDR values (estimated by
ERCOT) while the dashed lines show the predicted values derived from the estimated
spline regression model. As before, the primary independent variable is demand by Non4CP consumers expressed as a percentage of the monthly peak demand by these same
consumers. Year fixed effects are also included in the model, producing five parallel curves
(one per year) that account for differences in CPDR values over time.

Across the range of values shown in this figure, the model generally predicts higher CPDR
values as the measure ofNon-4CP demand increases. However, due to the quadratic nature

ofthis model, the upward curve that can be seen at the left-hand side ofthe graph continues
as the Non-4CP demand measure decreases (i.e., the model predicts higher CPDR values
at lower Non-4CP demand values). As such, while this model better accounts for nonlinearity in the relationship between CPDR and Non-4CP demand, it may be inappropriate
for use in predicting CPDR when Non-4CP demand is much lower.

Random Forest: A commonly used ensemble machine learning method that aggregates

the results of multiple decision tree models to produce a more robust, generalizable model.
To understand how a random forest functions, it is helpful to first describe the behavior of
an individual decision tree. Each decision tree in the random forest is a stand-alone

predictive model that repeatedly partitions the data into increasingly homogeneous subsets
based on observed relationships in the data. The data partitioning process ends when the

tree reaches a stopping condition , resulting in a set ofterminal nodes ( also known as leaves)
each ofwhich contains a relatively small and homogeneous subset ofthe data. The resulting

data partitioning rules and the leaves can then be used to make predictions.
Figure 12 depicts this partitioning process for a simple, illustrative decision tree. The tree

starts at the root node with the full dataset, where it first splits the data by year based on
whether an observation occurred before a threshold value, yeari Each of the resulting

subsets are then further partitioned at subsequent decision nodes based on the value of some
numerical variable, x. On the left-hand side of the tree, the model splits the data at a
threshold value x1, and on the right-hand side, it splits the data at a (potentially different)
threshold value x2. This repeated partitioning process isolates increasingly similar
observations, resulting in the leaf nodes (Leaf 1 through Leaf 4) which contain the final

subsets used for prediction.
A trained decision tree can be used to make a prediction for an out-of-sample observation
by applying the same sequence of decision rules learned during the training ofthe tree until
it reaches a leaf. At that point, the model outputs a prediction for the dependent variable
that is equal to the mean value of observations from the training dataset that are contained
in the corresponding leaf. In a random forest, which consists of multiple decision trees,

22

each tree produces its own leaf-based prediction, and the final model prediction is equal to
the average of the individual tree predictions.

As with the spline regression model discussed previously, training the random forest model
begins with selecting appropriate hyperparameters using an algorithm that conducts an
exhaustive search over every combination of a specified set of values. Several key

contain for the model to consider further splitting that subset of the data, and the minimum
number of observations that must be present in each of the terminal (leaf) nodes. This
algorithm compared the performance of each candidate model to determine the
hyperparameters that provide the best predictive accuracy, resulting in the following
combination: 250 estimators (trees), a requirement that a node contains at least two
observations to consider a split, and a minimum of at least 10 observations per leaf.

Monte-Carlo Simulation Overview - Within the simulation, several parameters that are likely
to affect the annual cost assessment are varied to study the relative impacts of different values for
each parameter. Table 10 provides a brief description of each parameter and the range of values
examined within the simulation. Each scenario in the simulation is defined by a unique
combination of parameters (cost assessment methodology, CPDR imputation model, CPDR

distributed error term, cost assessment outcomes are stochastic. As a result, each run of a scenario
produces varying demand reduction, and therefore, different cost shares for each customer class.
Each scenario is simulated 25 times to ensure the final dataset is balanced and contains a sufficient
number of observations to perform sound statistical analysis.

•

Cost Assessment Methodology -Directly affects cost shares by defining the days on which
demand reduction is calculated and how costs are apportioned between customer classes.
This parameter acts as the primary treatment variable for the simulation. Subsequent

CPDR Imputation Model - ¥ or any given day, the CPDR values predicted by each
imputation model will almost certainly differ. Additionally, the MSE of each model differs,
which affects the normally distributed error terms added to the predicted values. Using
multiple imputation models, that fit to the historical data in different ways, increases
robustness of the results by reducing reliance on any single approach.
CPDR Imputation Threshold - Sets the condition that determines for which days a CPDR
value is imputed. A higher CPDR threshold results in fewer days where CP consumers
engage in CPDR, which can affect the timing of coincident peaks and the ultimate cost
assessment. The lower value of 80% was selected for two reasons. First the historical data

on which the imputation models are trained primarily includes days above 80%, and
therefore, the imputation models are less accurate and realistic below this level. The higher

value of 90% was selected to provide a case that focuses attention on the types of days
where the majority of historical CPDR behavior has been observed.

during the four 15-minute CP intervals during the summer months. Variation in this
parameter allows for an examination ofthe effects of cost assessment methodologies based

timing of CPs across hours or even days.
Year - The possible values for this parameter are restricted by the calendar years for which
ERCOT and TDU data were collected. Simulating across multiple years provides value by
allowing the simulation to capture some level of inter-year variation in demand, which may
also reflect variation in other conditions (e.g., temperature). As shown in Figure 9,
historical CPDR, which can also clearly have a substantial effect on the annual cost share
outcomes, varies considerably across years.

The following points describe technical details of the simulation that merit additional discussion:

•

Determining CPDR Days - Within the simulation , CPDR values are only imputed for
days outside the current 4CP months (June, July, August, September). This approach
assumes that load data for these months already reflect any existing CPDR behavior, and
therefore, further estimation is unnecessary. Additionally, analysis of historical CPDR
behavior discussed previously highlights that CP customers do not respond to the same
degree on every day, but rather their response is related to the likelihood that a CP interval
will occur. To reflect this observation within the simulation, CP consumers are assumed to
only engage in CPDR for days that are within some range of the estimated coincident peak
and the corresponding aggregate response depends on the proximity to the actual CP
interval or hour.
However, the actual coincident peak can change based on the imputed level of demand
reduction. Consequently, within the simulation, CP consumers effectively use Non-CP
customer demand (Residential + Non-Residential) as a proxy for estimating the coincident
peak. This approach was informed by analyses described previously demonstrating a

27

relationship in the historical data between Non-CP customer demand and CPDR behavior.
Residential and Non-Residential customers are assumed to be inflexible and nonresponsive within the model.
In summary, CPDR values are only imputed for days that are within some percentage of
the estimated coincident peak (based on aggregate demand by Non-CP consumers). For
instance, for a simulation scenario with a CPDR threshold of 80%, CPDR values are only
imputed on days where Non-CP consumer demand is at least 80% of the maximum value
of Non-CP consumer demand for the corresponding period.

•

Overlapping CP Periods - Some cost assessment methodologies include CP periods that
overlap with one another. For example, the 5CP (4CP + FS) case includes the traditional
4CP months as well as a floating summer CP, which identifies a single peak over the entire
summer Oune-September). In this case, because the floating summer period shares days
with the fixed month CPs, it is considered an overlapping period. The simulation is
designed to ensure that overlapping periods do not result in repeated coincident peak days.
Without this design choice, the floating summer period would choose one ofthe coincident
peaks already selected by one of the fixed month CPs, which would create no additional
incentive to respond and have no effect on the resulting cost assessment.
In all scenarios, even if there are overlapping periods, CPDR values are calculated once
per day. If a CPDR value was already imputed for a day while estimating response for

another period, the simulation does not recalculate values even ifthat same day falls within
an overlapping period. Thus, the presence of overlapping periods can introduce new

demand response days, but it cannot change any preexisting demand response behavior.
Ideally, demand response estimation during overlapping periods would be informed by a

formal game-theoretic analysis, but the above approach was chosen for simplicity and to
minimize the number of assumptions built into the model.
•

Cost Assessment Calculation - Once demand reduction values have been imputed ,
aggregate demand (by all consumers) is adjusted and coincident peaks for each period are
determined. Annual cost shares are calculated based on consumer demand during the
coincident peaks corresponding to the cost assessment methodology for that scenario.

This section concludes by providing an overview ofthe flow ofthe Monte Carlo simulation, which
is shown visually in Figure 15. At a high level, the simulation can be divided into three steps. First,
the historical consumer demand data is passed to the simulation. Then, CPDR values are imputed
by identifying demand response days and estimating the aggregate amount of CPDR on each day
using the statistical models described previously. Finally, cost shares for each customer class are
determined by adjusting aggregate demand by the imputed level of CPDR, identifying relevant CP
periods, and measuring consumer demand during each peak period. For each simulation run, the

primary output is a cost assessment share for each customer class.

Simulation Results - This section presents results summarizing the simulation outcomes, with

an emphasis on how variation across different cost assessment methodologies affects each
customer class's share of costs. All results described in this section are presented on a percentage
scale describing the share of costs assessed to each customer class. To help put these results into
perspective, recall that, in the available data, total WTSC from 2020-2024 were approximately
$13.33 Billion. Therefore, WTSC averaged approximately $3.27 Billion annually and a one
percentage point share equates to just over $32.65 Million. The cost shares described in this section

are normalized such that they represent a share of the total costs represented within the data
provided by TDUs (recall that, on average, this data represents approximately 70% of ERCOTwide demand).

29

Another important consideration in the interpretation of these results is that the estimated effects
described are likely conservative. CPDR behavior within the simulation is imputed using data on
historical CPDR behavior under the existing 4CP methodology. As a result, the simulation cannot
account for any potential dampening effect on CPDR behavior that may arise when the number of
CPs assessed is increased. Specifically, as more intervals are included in the cost assessment
methodology, it may be the case that the amount of CPDR observed decreases, whether due to CP
consumers becoming less responsive overall as the number of CP periods assessed increases or
due to the increased complexity of having to correctly time demand response in more instances. If
there is an aggregate dampening effect , then the simulation results would tend to overstate the
amount of CPDR , and as a result , understate the amount of demand ( and cost share assessment)
by CP consumers. In turn, the share of costs assessed to Residential and Non-Residential
consumers would tend to be 0¥erstated.

B.

Impacts to wholesale market

Section I. C. of this report discusses potential wholesale market impacts associated with the current
4CP methodology, focusing on how an out-of-market financial incentive may influence observed
load patterns and wholesale energy price outcomes. Any mechanism that is based on a coincident
peak methodology for transmission cost recovery likely creates incentives for customers to reduce
demand during targeted intervals, and therefore, introduces a risk of suppressing energy prices
during those periods. In general, the wholesale energy price impacts under any alternative cost
assessment methodology will depend on whether the methodology shifts the timing of demand
reductions or changes the strength ofthe incentive to reduce demand.
As shown in Figure 6, elevated energy prices in recent years have tended to occur during periods
of higher net load, which does not necessarily align with peak gross load. If an alternative cost

assessment methodology incentivized additional load reductions during periods of high energy
prices (such as hours with high net load), it may further increase the difference between actual
energy prices and the energy prices that would have occurred absent this out-of-market incentive.
Conversely, if incentivized load reductions were to occur primarily during hours that are
increasingly not associated with generation scarcity, the resulting wholesale energy price impacts
would be expected to be smaller.
More broadly, if the number of CP intervals included in the cost assessment methodology were
increased, there would be more instances during which CP-incentivized demand response may
impact wholesale market energy prices. However, it may also be the case that the incentive

associated with reducing load in any single interval is dampened, because the benefit of curtailing
demand would be spread across a larger set of intervals. On the other end of the spectrum,
eliminating the demand response incentives associated with CP based methodologies would likely
bring back avoided load, but the effect on energy prices would depend on whether that load returns
during generation scarcity conditions.
Finally, transmission cost assessment is fundamentally a cost-recovery and cost-assignment
mechanism, not a wholesale market design tool. Accordingly, alternative cost assessment

methodologies should be evaluated primarily on ratemaking principles rather than strictly on their
impacts on wholesale energy prices. These effects are best considered secondary and potentially

unintended, rather than goals of the cost assessment methodology.

Nonbypassable costs

§ 37.0561 contributes to the interconnecting electric utility's costs to interconnect the large load

customer to a utility's system are paid by the large load customer typically through contribution in
aid of construction (CIAC), and therefore, are not included in TCOS and wholesale transmission
service at transmission voltage (WTS-TV) rates collected from the broader body of ratepayers.
Project No. 58481 was opened on July 31, 2025 to implement PURA §37.0561 and is the

project is not necessary to specifically implement PLJRA § 35.004(c-1). Commission Staff

B.

Separate rate treatment for large loads and flexible large loads

The magnitude of load in a small geographic area associated with large load customers raises
important issues associated with the existing transmission cost recovery methodology. Importantly,

large loads are likely to need significant and extensive transmission upgrades to adequately serve
them. Even though a single large load customer may be fully capable of eliminating electric
consumption during the 4CP or successor intervals, the probable need for system upgrades beyond

typical interconnection costs may warrant a distinct but temporary cost recovery treatment for such
customers based on some type of a demand metric. For example, large load customers could, for
some period determined by the Commission, be billed at retail 4CP or successor interval rates on

a nonbypassable basis based on the large load customer's maximum contracted peak demand.
In order to equitably reflect this retail rate treatment at the wholesale level, DSPs would need to
give ERCOT access to the necessary metering data for all large load customers in the ERCOT
region, and in turn, ERCOT would include both actual and maximum contracted load of large load
customers by DSP in ERCOT's annual 4CP or successor data report to the Commission, for use in
wholesale transmission cost recovery. Similarly, investor owned utility (IOU) DSPs would be

required to correspondingly allocate costs to large load customers based on maximum contracted
demand, and to design rates for large load customers based on that same demand.
This approach would ensure that large load customers' transmission charges better reflect the
uniquely disproportionate costs they cause to be imposed on the system. DSPs serving large load

customers would pay wholesale transmission charges as if its large load customers were on the
system at the time of the 4CP or successor intervals. IOU DSPs would then allocate their
transmission costs to the transmission rate class as if the large load customers were at maximum
load at the peak intervals, and thereby avoid allocating those costs to other rate classes. Requiring

such treatment for large load customers for a period of ten or fifteen years would ensure that large
load customers pay a significant portion of the system upgrade costs they cause to be incurred, as
well as their use of the existing transmission system. After such a time period, organic load growth
would presumably develop to utilize some or all ofthe associated transmission buildout, and large
load customers would revert to the standard 4CP or successor rate design based on their actual
metered load in order to realize the benefits offered by that rate design.

Project No. 58481, Rulemaking to Implement
Interconnection Standards Under PURA § 37.0561

Large

PURA § 37.0561 requires the Commission to establish standards for interconnecting large load
customers in the ERCOT region in a manner designed to support business development while
minimizing the potential for stranded infrastructure costs and maintaining system reliability.
PURA § 37.0561 also requires the Commission to ensure that the standards include uniform
financial requirements for the development of transmission infrastructure needed to serve a large
load customer. Consistent with these legislative directives and the legislative directives in PURA
§ 35.004(c-1), Commission Staff opened Project No. 5848122 to establish interconnection
standards for large load customers. In Project No. 58481, Commission Staff aims to standardize
across all electric utilities in the ERCOT region the financial commitments and the direct
interconnection costs that large load customers must pay.
Historically, electric utilities in the ERCOT region have taken different approaches to determining

the financial commitments and the direct interconnection costs that large load customers must pay.
Some electric utilities require a large load customer to post financial security for services, such as
engineering studies, or for equipment that has a long-lead time for procurement. Other electric
utilities have no such requirement. With respect to direct interconnection costs, some electric
utilities offer an allowance. In these circumstances, the large load customer pays only the direct
interconnection costs that exceed the allowance. This patchwork approach across electric utilities
in the ERCOT region has resulted in some service areas being viewed as more favorable for siting
and has contributed to difficulties in accurately forecasting load in the ERCOT transmission
planning process. Additionally, to the extent that not all direct interconnection costs are treated the
same, ratepayers across the ERCOT region are bearing more of the direct interconnection costs for

transmission infrastructure than they should be.
One ofthe ways the pending rule in Project No. 58481 is expected to standardize the requirements
for financial commitments is by requiring a large load customer to post significant financial
security on a dollar per MW basis. A second way is by requiring a large load customer to post
security for significant equipment or services and for system upgrades. Significant equipment is
that equipment that has a long-lead time of six months or more. Additionally, the pending rule will
address the circumstances that security may be refunded and whether a certain amount of security

should be non-refundable.
Finally, the pending rule is expected to establish a non-refundable interconnection fee and
standardize the requirements for CIAC, including allowances, if any. CIAC is a direct cash
payment to the utility for the costs to interconnect a large load customer. While CIAC has
traditionally been limited to the interconnection costs that exceed TSP-specific standard allowance

amounts and is typically characterized as "driveway costs" to denote that the interconnecting large
load customer is the sole beneficiary of the infrastructure, the pending rule evaluates whether the

On its face, expanding the interconnection costs to include system upgrades is an appealing

Docket No. 58923, Commission Staff's Petition to Require Annual
Updates to Class Allocation Factor Values Under 16 TAC §25.193(c)

The transmission cost recovery factor (TCRF) proceeding, discussed above, is available to IOU
DSPs and governed by 16 TAC § 25.193, relating to Distribution Service Provider Transmission
Cost Recovery Factor (TCRF). TCRF proceedings allow IOU DSPs that are billed for transmission
service by a TSP to charge or credit the DSP's customers for the wholesale transmission charges
approved or allowed by the Commission. Because the TCRF proceeding reflects transmission
charges that the Commission has already authorized, this type of proceeding is essentially a "passthrough" mechanism that enables a DSP to recover transmission charges that the Commission has
previously approved for TSPs to bill DSPs. A DSP may file up to two TCRF proceedings per year.
TCRF rates are calculated using baseline class allocation factor values for each customer class.
The class allocation factor values are approved for use in the DSP's most recent comprehensive
rate proceeding. Under 16 TAC § 25.247, an electric utility must file an application for a

comprehensive rate review within 48 months of the order setting rates in its most recent
comprehensive rate proceeding. Thus, the class allocation factor values are fixed between
comprehensive rate proceedings and generally updated on a four-year cycle. The transmissionvoltage class load has been growing at a disproportionate rate to other classes. As an example, AEP

estimates that its transmission voltage class load will be over four times the size of residential by
2030.23 This disproportionate growth combined with the four-year cycle for updating cost

allocation factor values results in significant inequitable cost-shifting between customer classes.
The TCRF formula's default use of the class allocation factor from the most recent base rate

proceeding, in combination with the use of updated billing determinants reflecting customer
growth, leads to inequitable cost-shifting between customer classes and large swings in rates when
allocation factor values are updated in a base rate proceeding. With the widespread deployment of
Advanced Metering Systems (AMS), annual updates to the class allocation factor values used in

TCRF proceedings may reasonably be implemented to address the inequitable cost shifting and
rate volatility. The current TCRF rule language fixing allocation factor values between rate cases
was based on an era without widespread AMS deployment, when updating class allocation factor
values involved contentious and highly contestable load research studies. These studies required

establishing customer samples and applying various statistical assumptions and techniques to
estimate class loads, and such practices would be inconsistent with the routine and streamlined
nature of the TCRF proceedings. These conditions no longer hold, and AMS systems can now
provide accurate class load values for all customers with AMS meters, without the need for any

significant degree of sampling or statistical estimation.
Where a TCRF rate class is experiencing disproportionate load growth, as has been the case for
many classes that include industrial customers in recent years, the failure to update the TCRF class
allocation factor values in the biannual TCRF updates results in the same outdated and fixed
proportion of TCRF costs being allocated to that class despite the load growth. As the load growth
causes a higher amount of wholesale transmission costs to be charged to the DSP, the failure to

update the allocation factor values annually results in other rate classes inequitably bearing a large
portion ofthe costs associated with the disproportionate load growth. Annually updating the TCRF

class allocation factor values would avoid this inequitable outcome, as the updated class allocation
factor values would align the class allocations with the cost-causative 4CP demand that causes the
DSP to incur wholesale transmission charges under the current wholesale transmission rate design.
Unlike with the class allocation factor values, the TCRF rule does currently require updates to the
billing determinant values used to establish the TCRF rates. This aspect of the rule, interacting
with the fixed allocation factor value issue discussed above, means that a rate class demonstrating
disproportionately high load growth faces significant downward pressure to its TCRF rates
between base rate proceedings, as the allocation factor value used to allocate TCRF costs to the
class is fixed, but the billing determinant denominator value used to calculate the rate increasesleading to rates being established significantly below cost for customers of these fast-growing
classes. This in turn can lead to increasingly lower TCRF rates for these fast-growing classes with
each TCRF update until the TCRF class allocation factor values are updated in the DSP's next rate
case. This dynamic has produced the situation where, as part of a base rate proceeding, the fastgrowing classes abruptly face very large rate increases as the allocation factor value update
"catches up" to the load growth and billing determinant values and abruptly corrects the
inequitable intra-class cross-subsidization caused by failing to update the class allocation factor
values on an annual basis. In other words, the status-quo contributes to significant rate volatility,
in addition to the equity issues discussed above.
Currently, the Commission has a pending docket in which Commission Staff has petitioned the
Commission to require annual updates to class allocation factor values under 16 TAC
§ 25.193(c).24 However, an additional option for resolving this issue is to update 16 TAC § 25.193
to require annual updates to class allocation factor values.
