- **How it works #1** (cites e8, e4, e5, e16, e33): The IMM says solar production peaks in the middle of the day. [e5] The IMM's Table 2 lists, for solar, overall, daily-peak-load and daily-net-peak capacity factors of 22.6%, 40.4% and 10.1% for 2023, 22.2%, 42.5% and 3.9% for 2024, and 23.8%, 48.4% and 1.7% for 2025 (the second set of three columns in the table, which matches the 2025 text). [e16][e4] The IMM defines the capacity factor as the ratio of a resource's energy output to its maximum capability. [e33]
- **How it works #2** (cites e3, e2, e18): The IMM says solar resources are more likely to be curtailed at midday, when simultaneous production peaks. [e3] Roughly 6% of solar generation was curtailed in 2025, which the IMM calls relatively low, partly because solar projects can be sited closer to load and are less prone to congestion than wind. [e2] The IMM says renewable developers also expand into regions with lower renewable potential but less congestion, a strategy it calls particularly applicable when capital cost declines, as it has for solar, even if annual output is lower. [e18]
- **How it works #3** (cites e27, e25, e26, e24): ERCOT's CDR treats solar capacity through an Effective Load Carrying Capability (ELCC), which is not a direct measure of generation output but an indicator of the expected reliability benefit of the capacity. [e27] The ELCC capacity is the ELCC percentage multiplied by the resource type's aggregate installed capacity in MW, and ELCCs are estimated by year, season and daily risk period (afternoon and evening for non-winter months; morning, afternoon and evening for winter months). [e25][e26] For solar, the CDR says ELCC falls as solar is added: the risk of reliability issues is pushed into the evening hours when solar drops, and solar ELCCs decrease sharply for the evening risk period but also for the afternoon. [e24]
