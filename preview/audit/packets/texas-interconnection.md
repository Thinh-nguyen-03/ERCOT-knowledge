## Statements to check (new or changed since the last audit)

- **Definition #1** (cites e1, e13, e15, e2, e3): The ERCOT System (the FERC-NERC report calls it the "ERCOT Interconnection"; FERC's Energy Primer calls it the Texas Interconnection) is the interconnected power system under PUCT jurisdiction that is not synchronously connected to the Eastern Interconnection or the Western (WECC) grid. [e1][e13][e15] A DC tie is a non-synchronous transmission interconnection between ERCOT and a non-ERCOT system, and the FERC-NERC report describes four asynchronous ties. [e2][e3]
- **How it works #1** (cites e15, e3, e4, e5) FLAG number-not-in-quote: 2021: FERC's staff primer describes the national grid as three main sections, the Western, Eastern and Texas Interconnections, which operate independently with limited interconnections between them. [e15] The 2021 FERC-NERC report describes ERCOT as a functionally separate interconnection with four asynchronous ties. Two DC ties, the North Tie and the East Tie, connect it to the Eastern Interconnection through SPP. [e3] Two more, the Laredo Variable Frequency Tie and the Railroad Tie, connect it to Mexico. [e4] Total DC tie capability is about 1,220 MW: 820 MW to SPP and 400 MW to Mexico. [e5]
- **How it works #2** (cites e6, e23, e18, e19, e20, e12) FLAG number-not-in-quote: 2021: Power can flow either way across the ties. Exports add to ERCOT demand and imports add to supply. [e6] Every DC tie schedule must follow the Protocols, NERC Reliability Standards, NAESB standards and ERCOT's operating agreements with CFE (current Protocol text; a pending NPRR857 change would generalize the counterparty). [e23] An importing QSE is treated as a Resource at the DC Tie Settlement Point; an exporting QSE is treated as a Load there and pays allocated losses, UFE and ERCOT fees. [e18][e19] e-Tags reported before 1430 Day-Ahead feed the DRUC as capacity supply or obligation; later ones feed the HRUC. [e20] The 2021 FERC-NERC report says ERCOT does not classify DC tie flows as firm capacity because they are scheduled as day-ahead energy transactions, although its seasonal assessment (SARA) did assume some net imports based on winter 2013/2014 EEA intervals. [e12]
- **How it works #3** (cites e21, e31, e25): Exports are the first to go in a pinch. DC Tie Load is counted as Load in reliability studies and settled as Adjusted Metered Load, but it is curtailed before other ERCOT Load and during EEA events. [e21] ERCOT may also curtail a schedule when the neighboring system operator asks because of an emergency in its own area. [e31] On the import side, using DC Tie import capacity not already in use is one of the prescribed EEA Level 1 steps. [e25]
- **How it works #4** (cites e7, e30): Isolation shapes reliability. With no synchronous neighbors, all frequency response must come from resources inside ERCOT. Because the system is smaller than the other interconnections, losing a unit causes a steeper frequency drop, so ERCOT needs more robust frequency response. [e7] DC ties energized after January 1, 2021, and ties modified by adding 20 MW or more of capacity or by a non-in-kind converter change, must meet the Operating Guide's frequency ride-through requirements. [e30]
- **Formula #1** (cites e24, e33, e34): The Real-Time payment for DC tie imports is priced at the Real-Time Settlement Point Price at the DC Tie Settlement Point: RTDCIMPAMT(q, p) = (-1) × RTSPP(p) × (RTDCIMP(q, p) × ¼), for each 15-minute Settlement Interval. [e24] In words: the QSE's aggregated DC Tie Schedule as an importer (in MW) for the interval, times one quarter to turn 15 minutes of MW into MWh, times the real-time price at that tie, times (-1). [e24][e33] The Protocols define RTDCIMPAMT as the payment to the QSE for the DC Tie import; the (-1) reflects the settlement sign convention for payments, which this section does not itself explain. [e34]
- **ERCOT context #1** (cites e8, e16, e9, e29): Isolation also shapes regulation. The FERC-NERC staff report states that ERCOT's wholesale market is not regulated by FERC because power exchanges between entities occur wholly within the state, not through interstate commerce. [e8] FERC's own primer says simply that FERC regulates all RTOs/ISOs except ERCOT. [e16] The Protocols back this up. ERCOT must act to keep entities in the ERCOT Region from becoming "public utilities" under the Federal Power Act, including by ordering ties to other Control Areas disconnected or by denying or curtailing e-Tags over DC ties. [e9] At state level, PURA requires a power region to establish an independent organization to ensure nondiscriminatory transmission access and the reliability and adequacy of the regional network. [e29]
- **ERCOT context #3** (cites e32, e27, e28, e11, e10, e13): Net DC tie flows are small and change direction. The IMM's 2020, 2022 and 2023 reports describe ERCOT as a net importer (2022 by a wider margin than 2021), in a trend they say began in 2018 because of tight supply and higher prices in 2018 and 2019. [e32][e27][e28] The IMM's 2024 report calls 2024 ERCOT's first net-export year "since 2018", so the IMM reports are not consistent about 2018 itself. [e11] In 2025 it returned to net importer, exporting to Mexico and importing from SPP, with average net inflow of only about 36 MW. [e10] During Winter Storm Uri, ERCOT imported what it could, but with 1,220 MW of tie capability it could not import the many thousands of MW that MISO and SPP brought in. [e13]
- **Key facts #2** (cites e5, e6, e26) FLAG number-not-in-quote: 2021, 2025, 2020: - About 1,220 MW of total tie capability: 820 MW to SPP and 400 MW to Mexico (FERC-NERC 2021 report and IMM 2025 report); the IMM's 2020 report described three Mexico ties totalling 430 MW. [e5][e6][e26]
- **Key facts #3** (cites e18, e19): - Importer = Resource, exporter = Load at the DC Tie Settlement Point; exporters pay allocated losses, UFE and fees. [e18][e19]
- **Key facts #4** (cites e21): - DC Tie Load is curtailed before other ERCOT Load and during EEAs. [e21]
- **Key facts #5** (cites e22): - Market Participants may not make DC tie exports reasonably expected to be uneconomic, excluding CARD and CRRBA allocations (a pending NPRR1030 box would delete this paragraph). [e22]
- **Key facts #7** (cites e32, e28, e11, e10): - Net flow: net importer through 2023 in a trend the IMM dates from 2018 (its 2024 report is inconsistent on 2018), net exporter in 2024, net importer in 2025 with about 36 MW average net inflow. [e32][e28][e11][e10]
- **Timeline #1** (cites e17): - 1941: ERCOT and SPP formed to pool resources for the war effort (FERC primer). [e17]
- **Timeline #2** (cites e32, e28, e11): - 2018: the start, per the IMM's 2020-2023 reports, of a net-import trend over the ties that continued through 2023 (the IMM's 2024 report instead implies 2018 was a net-export year). [e32][e28][e11]
- **Timeline #3** (cites e30): - January 1, 2021: DC ties energized after this date fall under the Operating Guide's frequency ride-through requirements. [e30]
- **Timeline #4** (cites e13) FLAG number-not-in-quote: 2021: - February 2021: Winter Storm Uri; ERCOT's tie import limit was 1,220 MW against nearly 13,000 MW imported by MISO and SPP. [e13]
- **Timeline #5** (cites e27): - 2022: net imports widened; the IMM attributes higher tie activity to high-summer-demand prices and the January 1, 2022 ORDC shift. [e27]
- **Timeline #6** (cites e11): - 2024: ERCOT a net exporter for the first time since 2018. [e11]
- **Timeline #7** (cites e10): - 2025: back to net importer, about 36 MW average net inflow. [e10]
- **Say it like this #1** (cites e5, e16, e8, e1): (Guidance) ERCOT is its own grid. It is tied to the rest of North America only through about 1,220 MW of DC ties, so in a crisis it has to rely almost entirely on resources inside Texas. [e5] Separately, FERC's primer says FERC regulates every RTO/ISO except ERCOT, and the FERC-NERC staff report explains that power exchanges there stay within the state; the system is under PUCT jurisdiction. [e16][e8][e1]
- **Common confusion #1** (cites e6, e14, e12, e21) FLAG number-not-in-quote: 2021: (Guidance) "Not synchronized" does not mean "not connected": the DC ties do move power, in both directions. [e6] ERCOT does not cover all of Texas. El Paso and parts of the panhandle and east Texas are outside it. [e14] Do not count tie imports as reliable capacity; the 2021 FERC-NERC report says ERCOT does not classify them as firm. [e12] And exports are not firm demand either: DC Tie Load is cut before other Load in an emergency. [e21] "SPP" in this topic is the Southwest Power Pool, not a Settlement Point Price.
- **Questions #8** (cites e32, e28, e11, e10): It changes from year to year: per the IMM, a net importer in a trend it dates from 2018 through 2023 (its 2024 report implies 2018 itself was a net-export year), a net exporter in 2024 and a net importer in 2025, with average net inflow of only about 36 MW in 2025. [e32][e28][e11][e10]
- **Questions #10** (cites e19, e21): The exporting QSE is treated as a Load at the DC Tie Settlement Point and pays allocated losses, UFE and ERCOT fees. [e19] DC Tie Load is curtailed before other ERCOT Load and during EEAs. [e21] (Reasoning) So an exporter selling into Mexico or SPP during a tight ERCOT evening carries curtailment risk that an ordinary ERCOT load does not.
- **Questions #12** (cites e31, e13): ERCOT may curtail a schedule when the neighboring operator requests it because of its own emergency. [e31] Uri showed both sides short at once: ERCOT imported what it could from the Eastern Interconnection, but only up to its 1,220 MW limit. [e13] (Reasoning) Ties help most when the neighbor has spare capacity, which is least likely in a region-wide cold snap.
- **Questions #14** (cites e12, e20): The FERC-NERC report says flows are scheduled as day-ahead energy transactions, not firm capacity. [e12] In the Protocols, an e-Tag reported before 1430 Day-Ahead enters the DRUC as capacity supply. [e20]
