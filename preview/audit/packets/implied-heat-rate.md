## Statements to check

- **Definition #1** (cites e1, e2) FLAG number-not-in-quote: 2024: The implied heat rate, as the IMM calculates it, is the power price divided by the fuel price, giving the heat rate that equates revenue from selling electricity with the cost of buying gas. [e1] In the 2024 report the IMM calls it the "implied marginal heat rate" and divides the real-time price of electricity by the natural gas price. [e2]
- **Formula #1** (cites e2, e1, e20, e19): Implied heat rate = real-time power price ($ per MWh) divided by the fuel price ($ per MMBtu), which gives a result in MMBtu per MWh. [e2][e1] The IMM's reports do not print the formula as an equation; the division is described in prose, and the unit follows from dividing $/MWh by $/MMBtu. For comparison, EIA writes the spark spread as power price minus natural gas price times heat rate, so the implied heat rate is the heat rate at which that spread is zero. [e20] A physical heat rate converts to efficiency by dividing 3,412 Btu (the Btu content of a kWh) by the heat rate. [e19]
- **How it works #1** (cites e6, e1): A heat rate is normally a measure of a generating station's thermal efficiency in Btu per kilowatthour. [e6] The implied version turns that around: it divides the market power price by the gas price to get the heat rate that equates revenue from selling electricity with the cost of buying gas. [e1] The IMM reads a high value as a less competitive market where even inefficient units profit, and a low value as a market where only the most efficient generators are in the money. [e1]
- **How it works #2** (cites e18, e22): The IMM uses it to separate fuel effects from everything else: real-time electricity prices are driven largely by natural gas prices but also influenced by other factors, and the implied marginal heat rate summarizes the changes related to those other factors. [e18] The 2025 report shows each month of 2025 and annual values for 2021 to 2025. [e22]
- **How it works #3** (cites e4, e9): Wind and solar push the number down by creating hundreds of hours at or below $0 per MWh, while shortage pricing pushes it up by creating hundreds of hours above gas marginal cost.
