Generators earn returns over many years, not annually, and periods of lower net revenues can naturally discourage new investment in the short term. ## Statements to check (new or changed since the last audit)

- **Definition #1** (cites e1, e2, e19, e20): An energy-only market relies mainly on expected shortage revenue in the energy and ancillary service markets to attract investment in new generation. [e1] ERCOT uses this design. It does not have a capacity market. [e2] FERC regulates all RTOs/ISOs except ERCOT, and FERC staff note that most RTOs/ISOs run a capacity market that lets LSEs meet their reserve obligation. [e19][e20]
- **How it works #2** (cites e4, e23, e24) FLAG number-not-in-quote: 2010: The core of the design is shortage pricing. When supply is tight, prices rise to reflect the higher value of available generation, and those spikes are how generators recover fixed costs. [e4] As the IMM described it in its 2010 report, the objective is to let energy prices rise far higher during legitimate shortages, when supply cannot meet both energy and minimum operating reserve requirements; outside shortages (most of the time), competitive outcomes are expected to look the same as in a capacity market. [e23] Investment then depends on two expectations: how high prices go in a shortage and how often shortages happen. The higher the shortage price, the fewer shortages are needed to give the same signal. [e24]
- **How it works #3** (cites e12, e15, e16): In ERCOT, before RTC went live in December 2025, that shortage pricing came from operating reserve demand curves, which set prices when the market lacked enough resources to meet its full energy and ancillary service requirements. If too few resources were being sustained, the signal could be strengthened by raising the value the ORDC implied. [e12][e15] The ORDC adder was discontinued in December 2025 with RTC, and the IMM says a set of sloped ancillary service demand curves now defines shortage pricing in the day-ahead and real-time markets. [e15][e16]
- **How it works #4** (cites e1, e10, e20): The design has a built-in tension. To produce investment revenue, the market has to go short on capacity, so energy-only markets can struggle to meet a 1-in-10 reliability standard. [e1] The IMM says meeting such a standard through shortage prices alone needs a very high value of lost load (VOLL), and that this is why most RTOs have adopted capacity constructs. [e10] In FERC staff's description, capacity markets are meant to give more certainty for investment in new capacity while letting all resources recover fixed costs over time. [e20]
- **How it works #5** (cites e8, e38, e15, e7, e17, e18, e41): There is also a brake on excess scarcity rent. The system-wide offer cap starts each year at the high cap (HCAP) and stays there until peaker net margin (PNM) passes three times CONE. [e8] If it does, the cap is set to the low cap (LCAP) for the rest of the calendar year, and energy prices, excluding congestion, may not exceed the LCAP plus $1. [e38] The rule text still says ERCOT will continue to apply the operating reserve demand curve and the reliability deployment price adder in that event, although the ORDC adder was discontinued in December 2025 with RTC. [e38][e15] The IMM puts the threshold at $315 per kW-year and describes the cut as $5,000 to $2,000 per MWh. [e7] That description does not mention the RTC split: the Protocol parameter table now lists the day-ahead cap at $5,000 and the real-time cap at $2,000 per MWh, and ERCOT says the SWCAP was reduced to $2,000 from $5,000 after RTC go-live on 12/5/2025. [e17][e18] A second brake comes from statute: the emergency pricing program must take effect if the HCAP has been in effect for 12 hours in a 24-hour period. [e41]
- **Formula #2** (cites e39, e8, e38, e30, e7, e17) FLAG number-not-in-quote: 25.509: Peaker net margin (16 TAC §25.509(b)): each day the peaking operating cost (POC) is 10 times ERCOT's natural gas price index, in $/MWh. From January 1, PNM accumulates (RTEP − POC) × (minutes in the settlement interval / 60) for every settlement interval in which the average system-wide real-time energy price (RTEP) exceeds POC. [e39] When the calendar-year total passes three times CONE, the cap drops to the LCAP. [e8][e38] The threshold uses the legacy CONE of $105 per kW-year, which gives the $315 per kW-year (315,000 $/MW-year) threshold. [e30][e7][e17]

Lean years discourage entry, which tightens supply and raises scarcity revenue, which then brings back investment.
