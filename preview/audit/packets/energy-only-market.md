## Statements to check

- **Definition #1** (cites e1, e2): An energy-only market relies mainly on expected shortage revenue in the energy and ancillary service markets to attract investment in new generation, with no separate capacity payment. [e1] ERCOT uses this design. It does not have a capacity market. [e2]
- **How it works #1** (cites e3): Generators fund their fixed and capital costs from net revenue, which is what remains of energy and ancillary service revenue after short-run operating costs. In ERCOT, those net revenues are the main economic signal for building and retiring plants. Day-ahead and bilateral forward revenue ultimately reflects expected real-time prices. [e3]
- **How it works #2** (cites e4, e12, e15, e16): The core of the design is shortage pricing. When supply is tight, prices rise to reflect the higher value of available generation, and those spikes are how generators recover fixed costs. [e4] In ERCOT, before RTC went live in December 2025, that shortage pricing came from operating reserve demand curves, which set prices when the market lacked enough resources to meet its full energy and ancillary service requirements. If too few resources were being sustained, the signal could be strengthened by raising the value the ORDC implied. [e12][e15] The ORDC adder was discontinued in December 2025 with RTC, and the IMM says a set of sloped ancillary service demand curves now defines shortage pricing in the day-ahead and real-time markets. [e15][e16]
- **How it works #3** (cites e1, e10): The design has a built-in tension. To produce investment revenue, the market has to go short on capacity, so energy-only markets can struggle to meet a 1-in-10 reliability standard. [e1] The IMM says meeting such a standard through shortage prices alone needs a very high value of lost load (VOLL), and that this is why most RTOs have adopted capacity constructs. [e10]
- **How it works #4** (cites e8, e7, e17, e18): There is also a brake on excess scarcity rent. The system-wide offer cap starts each year at the high cap (HCAP) and stays there until peaker net margin (PNM) passes three times CONE. [e8] The IMM puts the threshold at $315 per kW-year. Crossing it cuts the cap from $5,000 to $2,000 per MWh for the rest of that year, as the IMM describes it. [e7] That description does not mention the RTC split: the Protocol parameter table now lists the day-ahead cap at $5,000 and the real-time cap at $2,000 per MWh, and ERCOT says the SWCAP was reduced to $2,000 from $5,000 after RTC go-live on 12/5/2025. [e17][e18]
