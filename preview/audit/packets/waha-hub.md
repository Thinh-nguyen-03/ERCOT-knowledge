[e1] FERC's 2024 Energy Primer adds that since 2018 natural gas production has outpaced pipeline capacity in the Permian, which it ties to associated gas rising with crude oil production, and that the lack of transport infrastructure has frequently contributed to Waha prices falling below Henry Hub. [e11][e12]
- **How it works #2** (cites e2, e9, e10): EIA reported that Waha traded below zero on 42% of trading days in 2024, and that the Matterhorn Express Pipeline (2.5 Bcf/d, in service October 2024) helped clear part of the bottleneck; since mid-November 2024 the Waha price had been above zero as of EIA's February 2025 article. [e2] For scale, EIA reports the Henry Hub benchmark averaged $2.22/MMBtu in 2024 and $3.52/MMBtu in 2025. [e9][e10] The saved sources do not give Waha's own average price for either year.
- **How it works #3** (cites e14, e13, e16, e18): Pipeline additions have narrowed the gap before: FERC's primer says the Permian Highway, Whistler and Double E pipelines came online in 2021 and narrowed the price difference between Waha and Henry Hub. [e14][e13] The IMM's 2021 report likewise says the Waha-Katy separation lessened in 2021, and its 2022 report says it returned in 2022. [e16][e18]
- **ERCOT context #1** (cites e6, e15, e23, e4): ERCOT's own Fuel Index Price is based on Katy Hub gas, not Waha. [e6] The IMM says that since December 12, 2019 the Katy Hub replaced Houston Ship Channel as the FIP reference (NPRR952), and that ERCOT has flexibility to select the index used for the PNM threshold and LCAP. [e15] ERCOT must issue a Market Notice naming its selected source for the daily index prices used to calculate FIP. [e23] The IMM therefore treats the two hubs separately when it calculates net revenue by location: Katy prices for the Houston zone and Waha prices for the West zone. [e4]
- **ERCOT context #2** (cites e28, e24, e27, e25, e26): Where FIP enters offers. Offer curves carry a percentage of FIP and FOP for generation above LSL. [e28] The Mitigated Offer Cap for a Generation Resource takes the larger of two terms, the first being the generic incremental heat rate times the greater of FIP and the Resource's weighted average fuel price (WAFP), and the fuel price calculated for the second term is likewise the greater of WAFP or FIP plus a fuel adder. [e24][e27] WAFP is the volume-weighted average intraday, same-day and spot fuel price, or the projected incremental fuel consistent with a fuel supply contract, submitted to ERCOT in the Adjustment Period, and the fuel adder is the average cost above the index price the Resource has paid to obtain fuel. [e25][e26] (Reading of the formula: because FIP, a Katy price, sets a floor in these terms, a unit buying gas below Katy would not see that discount reflected in the formula's fuel price; the sources do not discuss this case.)
- **ERCOT context #3** (cites e3, e7, e29, e30, e21): Net revenue effect. The IMM says low Waha gas prices have historically produced much higher net revenues for gas resources in the West, and that the hub price gap widened in 2024 and 2025 as West zone congestion costs rose. The 2025 report attributes the 2025 widening to the Permian Basin import constraint and expects the gap to narrow as work on "these projects" concludes; the 2024 report tied the 2024 widening to transmission upgrades and maintenance in the West zone. [e3][e7] The IMM also says Waha prices dipped below $0 multiple times in 2021, 2022 and 2023 and were more volatile than Katy. [e29][e30][e21]

[e5] A cheap-gas West zone does not mean low West power prices: the IMM says West net revenues for gas units were much higher, with congestion costs also rising in the West. [e3] The IMM's net revenue comparison is a modeled estimate for new gas resources, not a measure of Waha-to-power price pass-through. [e17]
