## Statements to check

- **Definition #1** (cites e1, e2, e3, e4): Shape risk is the risk that comes from when a variable resource produces, hour by hour, rather than how much it produces in total. The sources describe it as uncertainty in the timing of renewable generation [e1], as power produced in varying amounts at different times and prices [e2], and as hour-to-hour generation that varies with wind and sun even when the total volume comes in as expected. [e3] The resulting mismatch is against the buyer's demand profile, against market prices, or both. [e2][e3][e4]
- **How it works #1** (cites e3, e2, e4): The sources agree that wind and solar output follows the weather, not demand, so its hourly profile rarely matches a buyer's load: WBCSD says a corporate buyer's demand profile is likely to be flatter, and Yale gives a data center using power day and night as an example. [e3][e2][e4]
- **How it works #2** (cites e24, e25, e5, e6): Prices vary through the day as well. In a virtual PPA the settlement each hour is the price difference times the volume in that hour, so the value depends on how output lines up with prices. [e24][e25] RMI says the intersection of uncertain generation with uncertain prices makes VPPA revenue uncertain, and that because generation and consumption rarely coincide, changing prices through the day can leave VPPA revenues short of offsetting the buyer's energy costs. [e5][e6]
- **How it works #3** (cites e8, e9): In a physical, as-produced PPA, shape risk shows up as balancing: when output falls short in an hour the buyer has to buy the difference, and when output exceeds need it has to sell. [e8][e9]
- **How it works #4** (cites e7, e3): The sources separate shape from volume. RMI says that timing can be weather-driven too, but volume risk is about the total amount; WBCSD says shape risk exists even if the total over a long enough period equals the estimate. [e7][e3]
- **How it works #5** (cites e20, e4, e14): EPEX SPOT, a European power exchange, adds a price-side effect: because solar plants produce at the same times, their combined output depresses prices in the midday hours and changes the shape of the price curve itself. [e20][e4] The IMM reports the same direction in ERCOT for captured prices (see ERCOT context). [e14]
- **How it works #6** (cites e21, e12, e23, e11): Mitigation in the sources: the buyer can size the contracted generation to its demand profile to reduce the hours and volumes it must buy or sell [e21]; buyers have shown interest in firm or shaped PPAs, physical or financial, that give a firm profile instead of the project's variable one [e12]; and POWER Magazine says a load-following product that matches generation to load hour by hour will likely need several resource types plus storage. [e23][e11]
- **ERCOT context #1** (cites e15, e16): The ERCOT data the IMM reports show why shape matters here. In 2025 most real-time price spikes occurred between 7 and 9 p.m., the peak net load hours when solar ramps down. [e15] The solar capacity factor in the daily peak net load interval fell to 1.7% in 2025, while wind held 29.3%. [e16]
- **ERCOT context #2** (cites e13, e14, e22) FLAG number-not-in-quote: 2025: The IMM defines the captured energy price as the weighted-average price paid to generators for the energy they produce. It says captured prices fall as installed renewable capacity rises, and that in 2025 solar's captured price ($21/MWh) was nearly at parity with wind's ($19/MWh) after years of a solar premium, a trend it expects to continue as solar shifts peak net demand into the evening. [e13][e14] It also says congestion lowers nodal prices in high-renewable regions. [e22]
- **ERCOT context #3** (cites e17, e18, e3): ERCOT's standard products are flat blocks. CRRs are auctioned in time-of-use blocks with the same MW in each hour: 5x16 and 2x16 blocks for hours ending 0700-2200 and 7x8 blocks for the remaining hours. [e17] The ICE ERCOT North peak futures contract uses the average of Settlement Point Prices for hours ending 0700-2200. [e18] A flat block hedges a constant MW, whereas a wind or solar profile varies within the block. [e17][e3]
- **Key facts #1** (cites e3, e7): - Shape risk is about timing of output within a period; volume risk is about the total. [e3][e7]
- **Key facts #2** (cites e3, e2): - A buyer's demand profile is usually flatter than wind or solar output. [e3][e2]
- **Key facts #3** (cites e6, e2): - In a VPPA, generation and consumption rarely coincide, so the hedge may not fully offset energy costs. [e6][e2]
- **Key facts #4** (cites e15): - In 2025, most ERCOT price spikes were between 7 and 9 p.m. as solar ramped down. [e15]

## Uncited guidance text (check for new facts, numbers, dates, names)

- (Example) Suppose (illustrative, made-up numbers) a solar project produces 10 MWh in a midday hour priced at $20/MWh and 2 MWh in an evening hour priced at $100/MWh, and a flat-load buyer uses 6 MWh in each hour. The project's captured price is (10 × $20 + 2 × $100) ÷ 12 = $33.33/MWh, while the simple average of the two hours is $60, a capture rate of about 56%. The buyer is long 4 MWh at midday (worth $80 if sold) and short 4 MWh in the evening (costing $400 to buy): the total volume matches its load, but the shape leaves it $320 worse off.
- (Say it like this) (Guidance) Shape risk is the gap between when your solar or wind farm produces and when you need power or when power is valuable. In ERCOT, solar produces at midday but prices spike in the evening ramp, so even a perfect annual volume match can leave you buying expensive evening power.
- (Common confusion) (Guidance) Shape risk is general industry usage and is not defined in the ERCOT Protocols, PUCT rules or IMM reports saved here.
- (Common confusion) Shape risk is not basis risk. Basis is about location; shape is about time. Both can hit the same contract.
