## Statements to check (new or changed since the last audit)

- **Definition #1** (cites e1, e2, e3, e27, e4, e34): Shape risk is the risk that comes from when a variable resource produces, hour by hour, rather than how much it produces in total. The sources describe it as uncertainty in the timing of renewable generation [e1], as power produced in varying amounts at different times and prices [e2], and as hour-to-hour generation that varies with wind and sun even when the total volume comes in as expected; Pexapark, which calls it profile risk, says the same. [e3][e27] The resulting mismatch is against the buyer's demand profile, against market prices, against contracted hourly sales, or a combination. [e2][e3][e4][e34]
- **How it works #1** (cites e3, e2, e4, e36): The sources agree that wind and solar output follows the weather, not demand, so its hourly profile rarely matches a buyer's load: WBCSD says a corporate buyer's demand profile is likely to be flatter, and Yale gives a data center using power day and night as an example. [e3][e2][e4] RFF gives the reverse case: a corporation that uses more power by day buying from a wind project that generates more at night. [e36]
- **How it works #2** (cites e24, e25, e38, e5, e6, e28): Prices vary through the day as well. RMI describes a VPPA's value in a period as the energy generated times the difference between market and contract price, and Norton Rose Fulbright and LevelTen say the calculation is typically made hour by hour, so the value depends on how output lines up with prices. [e24][e25][e38] RMI says the intersection of uncertain generation with uncertain prices makes VPPA revenue uncertain, and that because generation and consumption rarely coincide, changing prices through the day can leave VPPA revenues short of offsetting the buyer's energy costs. [e5][e6] Pexapark describes the same price side as the hourly profile, valued at hourly prices, being worth less (or more) than a baseload profile. [e28]
- **How it works #3** (cites e35, e36, e6, e34, e43, e31, e37): **Who carries it depends on the contract.** In an as-produced (as-generated) contract the buyer carries it: RFF says a synthetic PPA's volume is a share of actual generation, which removes the project's volume, shape and curtailment risks and leaves the counterparty bearing the mismatch in amount and timing. [e35][e36] RMI likewise places the shape mismatch in a VPPA on the buyer's side. [e6] In a contract with a fixed hourly volume, whoever committed to the volume carries it: RFF says a bank hedge's hourly fixed volume creates shape risk for the project, RMI that a fixed-volume price swap increases shape risk for the buyer that must provide the set amount, and Pexapark (writing on European baseload PPAs) that profile and volume risks sit with the seller, who must source any shortfall from the spot market. [e34][e43][e31] RFF adds that in a proxy revenue swap the counterparties bear the volume and shape risks. [e37]
- **How it works #4** (cites e8, e9, e40): In a physical, as-produced PPA, shape risk shows up as balancing: when output falls short in an hour the buyer has to buy the difference, and when output exceeds need it has to sell. [e8][e9] In a sleeved PPA, ORNL says the utility supplies additional grid electricity when renewable output does not meet the buyer's demand and takes on price-fluctuation risk while offering the buyer a fixed price. [e40]
- **How it works #5** (cites e7, e3, e27, e39): The sources separate shape from volume. RMI says that timing can be weather-driven too, but volume risk is about the total amount; WBCSD and Pexapark say shape (profile) risk exists even if the total over a long enough period equals the estimate. [e7][e3][e27] ORNL's DOE training lists volume and shape as separate VPPA risks. [e39]
- **How it works #6** (cites e20, e4, e29, e14): Two European sources add a price-side effect. EPEX SPOT says that because solar plants produce at the same times, their combined output increasingly affects clearing prices, especially between 10:00 and 16:00, and changes the shape of the price curve itself; Pexapark says that in high-renewable markets times of high wind or sun generally coincide with lower prices, the "cannibalization" effect, which it counts as part of profile cost. [e20][e4][e29] The IMM reports the same direction in ERCOT for captured prices (see ERCOT context). [e14]
- **How it works #7** (cites e32, e8, e9, e33, e10): On the load side, Montel (writing on European procurement) defines shape risk as the mismatch between a contracted baseload and the buyer's actual usage profile: usage above the baseload must be bought at a higher spot price, and unused energy sold for less. [e32] That is the same buy-and-sell balancing RMI and WBCSD describe for an as-produced PPA. [e8][e9] Montel says suppliers price the gap between actual and forecast consumption into a risk premium. [e33] FERC says more variable demand is typically more expensive to serve, especially when peaks coincide. [e10]
- **How it works #8** (cites e21, e12, e23, e42, e26, e11, e34): Mitigation in the sources: WBCSD says a buyer can size the contracted generation to its demand profile to reduce the hours and volumes it must buy or sell [e21]; WBCSD and POWER Magazine both report buyer interest in firm, shaped or hour-by-hour matched PPAs instead of the project's variable profile [e12][e23]; Pexapark says profile risk can only be hedged through fixed-price products, usually at a discount to baseload [e42]; WBCSD explores multi-technology PPAs as a way to a firmer profile [e26]; and POWER Magazine says a load-following product that matches generation to load hour by hour will likely need several resource types plus storage. [e23][e11] RFF says bank-hedge volumes are typically set at the project's hourly P99 generation to limit volume and shape risk. [e34]
- **Key facts #1** (cites e3, e7, e27): - Shape risk is about timing of output within a period; volume risk is about the total. [e3][e7][e27]
- **Key facts #3** (cites e6, e2, e36): - A mismatch between the generation shape and the buyer's consumption can weaken a PPA as a hedge; RMI says generation and consumption rarely coincide. [e6][e2][e36]
- **Key facts #4** (cites e35, e36, e34, e43): - As-produced contracts leave shape risk with the buyer; fixed hourly volumes leave it with whoever committed to the volume. [e35][e36][e34][e43]
- **Formula #1** (cites e13, e19, e30, e28): Price-side measures in the sources compare what a generator's output earns with the average price. The IMM defines the captured price as the output-weighted average price paid to generators [e13]; EPEX SPOT's Quality Factor (also called the capture rate) divides the achieved price by the baseload price, and Pexapark's capture factor is the same ratio of the volume-weighted average price to the baseload price. ## Uncited guidance text (check for new facts, numbers, dates, names)

- (Example) Suppose (illustrative, made-up numbers) a solar project produces 10 MWh in a midday hour priced at $20/MWh and 2 MWh in an evening hour priced at $100/MWh, and a flat-load buyer uses 6 MWh in each hour. The project's captured price is (10 × $20 + 2 × $100) ÷ 12 = $33.33/MWh, while the simple average of the two hours is $60, a capture rate of about 56%. The buyer is long 4 MWh at midday (worth $80 if sold) and short 4 MWh in the evening (costing $400 to buy): the total volume matches its load, but the shape leaves it $320 worse off.
- (Example) Suppose instead, illustratively, the project had sold a fixed 6 MWh in each hour. Now the project is short 4 MWh in the evening and must buy it at $100, and has 4 MWh to sell at $20 at midday: the same $320 shape cost sits with the seller.
- (Say it like this) (Guidance) Shape risk is the gap between when your solar or wind farm produces and when you need power or when power is valuable. In ERCOT, solar produces at midday but prices spike in the evening ramp, so even a perfect annual volume match can leave someone buying expensive evening power: the buyer under an as-produced deal, whoever committed to a fixed shape under a fixed-volume hedge.
- (Common confusion) (Guidance) Shape risk is general industry usage and is not defined in the ERCOT Protocols, PUCT rules or IMM reports saved here.
- (Common confusion) Shape risk is not basis risk. Basis is about location; shape is about time. Both can hit the same contract.
