## Statements to check

- **Definition #1** (cites e1): Transmission congestion arises when network power flows are restricted by limits on transmission facilities such as lines and transformers. When flow over a facility reaches its limit, the market shifts generation to higher-cost units to alter flows and serve load without exceeding the limit, so congestion keeps the lowest-cost generators from serving load. [e1]
- **How it works #1** (cites e2, e4, e6, e5): Congestion shows up in prices: differences in the cost of delivering electricity to different locations are reflected in different energy prices at each node. [e2] A constraint's marginal economic cost is reflected in its shadow price, which is defined as the rate at which system costs would fall or rise with a slight change in the amount of the commodity made available. [e4][e6] The IMM calculates day-ahead congestion values as power flow over each constraint times that constraint's shadow price. [e6] How much a given injection loads a constraint is its shift factor: the flow on a transmission element from a unit injection at a bus to a fixed reference bus. [e5]
- **How it works #2** (cites e7, e8): SCED caps the price of congestion: the cost it calculates to resolve an additional MW on a network constraint is limited to that constraint's maximum shadow price. [e7] Some constraints are generic transmission constraints (GTCs), which group transmission elements to limit flow between geographic areas to manage stability, voltage and other limits that cannot be modeled directly. [e8]
- **How it works #3** (cites e3, e14): Congestion rent is the difference between what consumers pay and what generators receive, based on locational price differences. It accrues to CRR holders. [e3] A CRR entitles its owner to be charged or paid congestion rent that arises when the grid is congested in the DAM or in real time. [e14]
- **ERCOT context #1** (cites e12, e9, e10): The IMM reports real-time congestion cost of $1.9 billion in 2024, down 17% from 2023. [e12] In 2025 day-ahead congestion rose 26%, ending a three-year decline; the main drivers were the North zone, where RUC commitments were used to manage congestion, and the West zone, where transmission has lagged renewables in the Panhandle and demand in the Permian Basin. [e9] Real-time congestion was 7% above day-ahead, which the IMM attributes to forecast error for load and renewables. [e10]
- **ERCOT context #2** (cites e11, e13): Violated-constraint hours fell to 64 in 2025, the lowest annual total ever, partly because NPRR 1230 lets SCED use more expensive dispatch to avoid violations. [e11] The IMM also notes that only about 9% of 2025 congestion cost was interzonal, so most of it occurs within load zones; the West zone is the largest contributor to the intrazonal share. [e13]

- **Common confusion #1** (cites e6, e3, e4, e2, e7, e11): Congestion cost is not the same as congestion rent: the first is a measure of the value of constraints, and the second is the difference between what consumers pay and generators receive, to which CRR holders have the financial right. [e6][e3] A shadow price is also not a price paid by an individual customer; it is a marginal value that feeds locational prices. [e4][e2] Binding and violated are different: the IMM treats violated constraints as a separate, shrinking count, and SCED's shadow price cap limits what it will pay to relieve a constraint. [e7][e11]
