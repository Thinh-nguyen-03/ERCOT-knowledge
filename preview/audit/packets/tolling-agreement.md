## Statements to check

- **Definition #1** (cites e1, e2, e25): A tolling agreement is a contract in which one party (the toller) supplies the fuel, usually natural gas, to another party's power plant and takes the electricity, which it then markets, in exchange for paying the plant owner a fee. [e1][e2][e25]
- **How it works #1** (cites e4, e5): The toller takes the market risk and the owner takes the plant risk. The SFU thesis says a toll typically assigns completion and performance risk to the developer or contractor and operating and gas and power price risk to the marketer. [e4] The Oil & Gas Journal article says plant sponsors give up the ability to make money on the spark spread and become responsible for the plant's performance, including its heat rate and availability. [e5]
- **How it works #2** (cites e5, e6, e7, e8, e18, e2): The owner is paid mainly in fixed amounts. The sources describe capacity payments made whether or not the plant runs, plus operating and maintenance payments and a start payment; the SFU thesis calls the monthly capacity charges "option premium costs", and the Fields slides describe monthly capacity payments rather than an upfront premium. [e5][e6][e7][e8] TotalEnergies describes the owner's position as stable revenue from fixed fees without exposure to gas or power price swings. [e18][e2]
- **How it works #3** (cites e11, e8, e19, e26): The toller gets a dispatch option and a physical position. It can choose day to day, even hour to hour, whether to run the plant and sell its power; the Fields slides describe daily day-ahead exercise into standard power buckets with monthly settlement. [e11][e8] The Oil & Gas Journal says the toll serves the tolling party as a physical asset hedge for its trading positions, and TotalEnergies says the toller uses plants without investing in building or maintaining them. [e19][e26]
- **How it works #4** (cites e6, e24): The owner's performance is enforced by contract. Sponsors face penalties for not meeting the toller's expectations, such as late construction, and availability may be guaranteed with liquidated damages depending on how the toll is structured. [e6][e24]
- **How it works #5** (cites e12, e13, e27, e28): Tolls are valued as options. Because the toller controls dispatch hourly or daily, the SFU thesis treats a toll as a strip of European spread options, and the Fields slides value each delivery day as the larger of zero and power minus heat rate times gas minus VOM. [e12][e13] More volatile power and gas prices raise the option (extrinsic or "volatility") value. [e27][e28]
- **How it works #6** (cites e9, e10): The main purpose, for the owner, is financing. The Fields slides say the primary purpose is to annuitize the value of an asset to be bought or built in order to borrow, and the SFU thesis describes the toll as a long-term offtake agreement on which the developer finances construction. [e9][e10]

TotalEnergies says tolling can apply to batteries, and an Aurora Energy Research presentation to ESIG describes battery tolls where the battery owner receives a pre-determined contract price and the offtaker receives dispatch rights and energy and ancillary service revenues. [e3][e16][e17][e15]

## Uncited guidance text (check for new facts, numbers, dates, names)

- (Example) Suppose (illustrative, made-up numbers) a toller pays $8/kW-month in capacity charges for a 500 MW combined cycle at a 7.0 heat rate and $3 VOM. That is $4 million a month whatever happens. In a 5x16 hour with power at $60 and gas at $3, the toller runs the plant and earns 60 - 21 - 3 = $36/MWh; at $20 power it leaves the plant off. The toller profits only if the hours it runs earn more than the fixed capacity charge.
- (Say it like this) (Guidance) "In a toll, the marketer rents the plant: it pays fixed capacity charges, supplies the gas, calls the plant when the spark spread pays, and keeps the power. The owner gets a steady payment to finance the plant and keeps the risk of the plant itself."
