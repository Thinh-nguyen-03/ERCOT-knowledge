## Statements to check (new or changed since the last audit)

- **Definition #1** (cites e1, e2, e25, e33) FLAG number-not-in-quote: 2012: A tolling agreement is a contract in which one party (the toller) supplies the fuel, usually natural gas, to another party's power plant and takes the electricity, which it then markets, in exchange for paying the plant owner a fee. [e1][e2][e25] The CFTC, summarizing how commenters described them in its 2012 swap-definition rule, says tolling agreements give a purchaser the right to the capacity, energy, ancillary services and other products of a specified generating unit, all based on a delivered fuel price and an agreed heat rate. [e33]
- **How it works #1** (cites e4, e5): The toller takes the market risk and the owner takes the plant risk. The SFU thesis says a toll typically assigns completion and performance risk to the developer or contractor and operating and gas and power price risk to the marketer. [e4] The Oil & Gas Journal article says plant sponsors give up the ability to make money on the spark spread and become responsible for the plant's performance, including its heat rate and availability. [e5]
- **How it works #2** (cites e5, e6, e44, e49, e7, e8, e2, e18) FLAG number-not-in-quote: 2020: The owner is paid mainly in fixed amounts. The Oil & Gas Journal article describes capacity payments made whether or not the plant runs, plus operating and maintenance payments and a start payment, and an Entergy model term sheet (a 2020 RFP form) sets out the same three: a Capacity Payment, a Variable Payment of a Variable O&M Rate times the energy dispatched by or for the Buyer, and a Start-up Payment per completed start, all paid monthly in arrears. [e5][e6][e44] A PacifiCorp form agreement likewise has the Buyer pay a monthly Capacity Payment. [e49] Separately, the SFU thesis calls the monthly capacity charges "option premium costs", and the Fields slides describe monthly capacity payments rather than an upfront premium. [e7][e8] TotalEnergies says the toller pays a conversion fee (a "tolling fee"), and that in its own TTEPH arrangement the plant-owning company receives fixed fees, giving it stable revenues without exposure to gas or electricity price swings. [e2][e18]
- **How it works #3** (cites e11, e8, e51, e46, e45, e48, e19, e26): The toller gets a dispatch option and a physical position. It can choose day to day, even hour to hour, whether to run the plant and sell its power; the Fields slides describe daily day-ahead exercise into standard power buckets with monthly settlement. [e11][e8] Resources for the Future, in a note on wind or solar projects with storage, also says a toll (which it calls common in fossil-fuel projects) lets the offtaker decide when the project dispatches. [e51] The contract forms show the same allocation: the Entergy term sheet lets the Buyer choose not to dispatch at all (0 MW) and makes the Buyer solely responsible for procuring and delivering fuel up to the gas delivery point, subject to the term sheet's other provisions; the PacifiCorp form has the Buyer supply the fuel, pay the Section 5 amounts, and receive the energy and ancillary services it schedules without additional charge. [e46][e45][e48] The Oil & Gas Journal says the toll serves the tolling party as a physical asset hedge for its trading positions, and TotalEnergies says its TTEPH arrangement lets TotalEnergies and EPH use the plants without investing in building or maintaining them. [e19][e26]
- **How it works #4** (cites e6, e24, e50, e47): The owner's performance is enforced by contract. Sponsors face penalties for not meeting the toller's expectations, such as late construction, and availability may be guaranteed with liquidated damages depending on how the toll is structured. [e6][e24] In the forms, availability feeds into payment: the PacifiCorp monthly capacity payment is multiplied by an availability adjustment factor, and the Entergy term sheet discounts the capacity payment when monthly availability falls below its requirement. [e50][e47]
- **How it works #5** (cites e12, e13, e27, e28): Tolls are valued as options. Because the toller controls dispatch hourly or daily, the SFU thesis treats a toll as a strip of European spread options, and the Fields slides value each delivery day as the larger of zero and power minus heat rate times gas minus VOM. [e12][e13] More volatile power and gas prices raise the option (extrinsic or "volatility") value. [e27][e28]
- **How it works #6** (cites e9, e10): The main purpose, for the owner, is financing. The Fields slides say the primary purpose is to annuitize the value of an asset to be bought or built in order to borrow, and the SFU thesis describes the toll as a long-term offtake agreement on which the developer finances construction. [e9][e10]
- **How it works #7** (cites e34, e54, e35, e16, e17, e36, e3): The same structure is used for batteries: in a physical toll the owner sells the right to operate the battery for a fixed payment (virtual tolls, below, differ). Modo Energy (writing about ERCOT), Troutman Pepper (a US law firm) and an Aurora Energy Research presentation to ESIG agree that the offtaker gets the right to charge, discharge or bid the battery, while the owner receives a fixed or pre-determined payment; Modo and Aurora also say the offtaker takes the energy and ancillary service revenues (Modo: all upside and downside from arbitrage and Ancillary Services); Troutman adds that the monthly payment often has adjusters based on project performance. ## Uncited guidance text (check for new facts, numbers, dates, names)

- (Example) Suppose (illustrative, made-up numbers) a toller pays $8/kW-month in capacity charges for a 500 MW combined cycle at a 7.0 heat rate and $3 VOM. That is $4 million a month whatever happens. In a 5x16 hour with power at $60 and gas at $3, the toller runs the plant and earns 60 - 21 - 3 = $36/MWh; at $20 power it leaves the plant off. The toller profits only if the hours it runs earn more than the fixed capacity charge.
- (Say it like this) (Guidance) "In a toll, the marketer rents the plant: it pays fixed capacity charges, supplies the gas, calls the plant when the spark spread pays, and keeps the power. The owner gets a steady payment to finance the plant and keeps the risk of the plant itself."
