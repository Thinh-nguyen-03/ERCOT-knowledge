- **Definition #1** (cites e40, e41, e42, e43, e53): In the CFTC's glossary, basis is the difference between the cash (spot) price of a commodity and the price of the nearest futures contract, which may reflect differences in time, product, grade or location, and basis risk is the risk of an unexpected widening or narrowing of that basis between the time a hedge is put on and the time it is lifted. [e40][e41] In power markets the word usually means a locational difference: LBNL calls the price difference between locations that congestion creates the "locational basis", and basis risk the risk that it moves against the buyer's or seller's position; Penn State likewise calls variation in the difference between two LMPs or zonal prices locational or "basis" risk. [e42][e43][e53]
- **Definition #2** (cites e1, e2, e8, e57, e3, e4, e5, e6, e62): In power contracts the sources describe it as the mismatch between the price a generator actually earns at its node and the price at the hub where its contract settles [e1][e2][e8][e57], or, from the buyer's side, between the generator's node and the price the buyer pays for its own power: its load zone price in RMI's guide, its retail price in Yale's. [e3][e4][e5] In natural gas, basis names the price differential between Henry Hub and another hub or delivery point. [e6][e62]
- **How it works #1** (cites e1, e2, e7, e60, e44, e9, e61): A generator sells its physical output at its node and is paid the nodal price, while a hub-settled contract pays or charges it against the hub price, so the two cash flows do not always line up. [e1][e2][e7][e60] LBNL describes the same gap from the market's side: a generator sells at its node while the load buys at a different node such as a liquid hub, and the gap between the generator's revenue at its node and the cost of buying the same amount, with the same output profile, at the hub is the generation-weighted basis. [e44] Norton Rose Fulbright puts it in contract terms (hub settlement requires the project to buy at the hub the same volume it sells at the node), and Akin Gump's ERCOT hedge slides say the same of a physically settled hedge (sale at the node, purchase at the hub for delivery). [e9][e61]
- **How it works #2** (cites e9, e13, e38, e57, e60, e10, e36): Several publishers say the project bears this node-to-hub mismatch under hub settlement. [e9][e13][e38][e57][e60] Two say the side carrying the risk loses when the node price falls relative to the price on the other side: the project when the hub is above its node (Norton Rose Fulbright), the buyer when the node falls relative to its retail price (Yale). [e9][e10][e36]
- **How it works #3** (cites e13, e14, e59, e9, e56, e58, e66): Hubs are aggregations of nodes; ReSurety describes a hub price as the average of the nodes in it. [e13][e14][e59] Two publishers say that because a hub covers a wider area, its price is less volatile than a single node's, so hub settlement is seen as less risky for the buyer. [e13][e14] The settlement point decides who carries the risk: settling at the hub puts the node-to-hub risk on the project, and settling at the node shifts it to the buyer. [e9][e13][e56][e58] Two publishers say the party that takes on more risk prices it in: Yale says the project then seeks a higher PPA price, and RFF that counterparties assuming more residual risk will likely apply a greater discount to hedge prices. [e13][e66]
- **How it works #4** (cites e42, e54, e11, e12, e43, e41, e6, e63): The sources agree that congestion is the cause in power. LBNL and RFF say the locational basis comes from transmission congestion that keeps prices from equalizing across the grid. [e42][e54] Yale and Norton Rose Fulbright give the renewable case: heavy wind or sun where many projects sit close together congests the lines and drives the node price down. [e11][e12] Basis risk exists because this difference changes over time: LBNL says a stable basis could simply be priced into the contract, but it varies with transmission capacity, generator output and load, which is the same idea as the CFTC's "unexpected widening or narrowing" of the basis during a hedge. [e43][e41] The FERC primer and EIA describe the gas version the same way: basis normally reflects transport cost and widens when pipelines are congested or takeaway capacity is limited. [e6][e63]
- **How it works #6** (cites e40, e67, e42, e53, e62, e64, e65): Three different spreads are called "basis", and they should not be mixed up. The futures meaning is cash (spot) price minus the nearest futures price [e40]; Montel uses it this way for power, the risk that the spot price at delivery does not line up with the forward contract. [e67] The locational power meaning is the difference between two LMPs: node versus hub, or between nodes or zones. [e42][e53] The gas meaning is the differential between Henry Hub and another delivery point, which FERC says is also traded as a basis swap or basis future paying on the difference between a location's month-ahead index and the Henry Hub settlement price. [e62][e64] RFF adds that a gas-based hedge for a power project carries gas basis risk, between the hub gas price and the local gas price. [e65]
- **ERCOT context #4** (cites e52, e50, e47, e48, e68, e49, e51): LBNL's study of MISO, SPP and ERCOT for 2015-2019 treats ERCOT's CRRs as the local name for FTRs. [e52][e50] It found that in ERCOT, unlike MISO and SPP, wind's basis risk was on par with that of gas-fired generators, and that in ERCOT and MISO the basis was generally positive for thermal plants, meaning they tend to sit where prices are above the hub. [e47][e48] Across the three markets, a fixed-volume FTR nearly matches a steady generator's basis but not a wind plant's varying output; it reduced wind's basis by $1-5/MWh but left $1.8-3.5/MWh on average, and in ERCOT it was also less effective for low-capacity-factor combustion turbines. [e68][e49] LBNL did not count the auction cost of the FTRs. [e51]
- **Key facts #8** (cites e40, e41): - CFTC: basis is typically calculated as cash minus futures; basis risk is an unexpected widening or narrowing of the basis while a hedge is on. [e40][e41]
- **Key facts #9** (cites e46, e52): - LBNL (MISO, SPP and ERCOT, 2015-2019): a wind plant contracted to sell at a hub in the central U.S. lost $2-8/MWh on average from basis alone, assuming congestion did not prevent delivery. This is a three-market figure, not an ERCOT-only one. [e46][e52]
- **Key facts #10** (cites e47) FLAG number-not-in-quote: 2015, 2019: - LBNL: in ERCOT, wind's basis risk was on par with gas-fired generators' (2015-2019). [e47]
- **Key facts #11** (cites e56, e58): - Node-settled contracts move basis risk from the project to the buyer. [e56][e58]
- **Formula #1** (cites e40, e42): Basis as a price is a difference between two prices, and the sign convention differs by source. [e40][e42]
- **Formula #2** (cites e40): - Futures (CFTC): basis = cash (spot) price - nearest futures price. [e40]
- **Formula #3** (cites e42, e45, e1): - Locational power basis (LBNL): node price minus hub price at the same time, so a negative basis means the plant's node is below the hub. [e42][e45] Norton Rose Fulbright speaks of "the difference between the hub price and the nodal price" without fixing a sign. [e1]
- **Formula #4** (cites e44, e9): - Generation-weighted basis (LBNL): the gap between what the generator earns selling its output at its node and what the same amount, with the same output profile, costs at the hub; in effect the node-minus-hub difference weighted by the plant's generation. This is the amount a hub-settled seller is exposed to. [e44][e9]
- **Formula #5** (cites e6, e62, e64): - Gas basis: the difference between the Henry Hub price and the price at another delivery point; a basis future pays on the difference between a location's month-ahead index and the Henry Hub LD1 settlement price. [e6][e62][e64]
- **Common confusion #5** (cites e40, e42, e45, e1): A futures glossary's "basis" is cash minus futures, a difference in time and contract as well as place, while a power desk's "basis" is usually node minus hub at the same moment. [e40][e42] Ask which one is meant, and which way round it is signed. [e45][e1]
- **Questions #10** (cites e26, e27, e28, e50, e68, e49, e51, e55): A CRR or PTP Obligation runs from a source Settlement Point to a different sink Settlement Point and pays or charges the sink-minus-source price difference, so a position from the project's node to the hub moves in the direction of the basis loss. [e26][e27] It settles on DAM prices, or on Real-Time prices for a PTP bought in the DAM, which may not match how the VPPA settles. [e26][e28] LBNL, which counts CRRs as FTRs, found that a fixed-volume FTR nearly hedges a steady thermal plant but leaves a wind plant with residual basis, because the plant's output varies and the FTR's volume does not; its figures ignore the auction cost. [e50][e68][e49][e51] RFF adds that it is hard to know how many transmission rights a project would need and that they typically run only one or two years. [e55]
- **Questions #14** (cites e40, e62, e64, e42, e53, e41, e43): To the CFTC it is cash minus the nearest futures price. [e40] In gas it is the differential between Henry Hub and another delivery point, traded as basis swaps or futures. [e62][e64] In power it is usually the locational difference between two LMPs, such as a generator's node and a hub. [e42][e53] Basis risk in each case is the risk that this difference moves unexpectedly while a hedge is on. [e41][e43]
- **Questions #16** (cites e56, e58, e35, e13, e66): Node settlement removes basis risk from the project but shifts it to the buyer. [e56][e58] Buyers and lenders tend to prefer liquid hubs, and the party left holding the risk wants to be paid for it in the price. [e35][e13][e66]

## Uncited guidance text (check for new facts, numbers, dates, names)

- (Example) Suppose (illustrative, made-up numbers) a 100 MW West Texas wind project has a hub-settled virtual PPA. In one hour it produces 100 MWh, the hub price is $60/MWh and its node price is $40/MWh because the area is congested. The project earns 100 × $40 = $4,000 selling at its node but is settled against 100 × $60 = $6,000 at the hub, so basis costs it 100 × ($60 − $40) = $2,000 for that hour. If instead the node were $65 and the hub $60, basis would add 100 × $5 = $500.
- (Say it like this) (Guidance) Basis risk is the gap between where you get paid and where your hedge settles. In ERCOT a renewable project is paid at its own node, so a hub-settled contract leaves it exposed whenever congestion pushes its node below the hub.
- (Common confusion) (Guidance) Basis risk is general industry usage and is not defined in the ERCOT Protocols, PUCT rules or IMM reports saved here.
