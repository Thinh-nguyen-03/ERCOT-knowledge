## Statements to check (new or changed since the last audit)

- **Definition #1** (cites e1, e2, e8, e3, e4, e5, e6): Basis risk is the risk that the price at one location moves differently from the price at the location a contract or hedge is tied to. In power contracts the sources describe it as the mismatch between the price a generator actually earns at its node and the price at the hub where its contract settles [e1][e2][e8], or, from the buyer's side, between the generator's node and the price the buyer pays for its own power: its load zone price in RMI's guide, its retail price in Yale's. [e3][e4][e5] In natural gas the same word, basis, names the price differential between Henry Hub and another hub. [e6]
- **How it works #1** (cites e1, e2, e7, e9, e13, e38, e10, e36): A generator sells its physical output at its node and is paid the nodal price, while a hub-settled contract pays or charges it against the hub price, so the two cash flows do not always line up. [e1][e2][e7] Two publishers say the project bears this node-to-hub mismatch. [e9][e13][e38] Two say the side carrying the risk loses when the node price falls relative to the price on the other side: the project when the hub is above its node (Norton Rose Fulbright), the buyer when the node falls relative to its retail price (Yale). [e9][e10][e36]
- **How it works #2** (cites e13, e14, e9): Hubs are aggregations of nodes. Two publishers say that because a hub covers a wider area, its price is less volatile than a single node's, so hub settlement is seen as less risky for the buyer. [e13][e14] Settling at the hub puts the node-to-hub risk on the project. [e9][e13]
- **How it works #3** (cites e11, e12, e6): Both publishers that explain the cause point to congestion: heavy wind or sun where many renewable projects sit close together congests the transmission lines and drives the node price down. [e11][e12] The FERC primer describes the gas version the same way: basis normally reflects transport cost and can widen sharply when pipelines between two points are congested. [e6]
- **How it works #4** (cites e2, e7, e9, e3, e5): Basis risk is the same structure seen from two sides. For the project in a hub-settled deal it is node versus hub. [e2][e7][e9] For a buyer using a virtual PPA as a hedge it is the correlation between the project's price and the buyer's own price; when the two stop moving together the hedge erodes. [e3][e5]
- **ERCOT context #1** (cites e19, e20, e21): ERCOT has three kinds of Settlement Point: a Resource Node, a Load Zone, or a Hub. [e19] Generators settle at their own node's LMP, while loads are billed at the load zone price, a load-weighted average of nodal prices in the zone. [e20] Except for the ERCOT Hub Average 345 kV Hub, a Hub's real-time LMP is the arithmetic average of the LMPs of its Hub Buses. [e21] So a generator paid at its node and a contract written at a hub or load zone are, by construction, priced at different Settlement Points.
- **ERCOT context #2** (cites e22, e23, e24): The IMM explains locational price differences in ERCOT as the result of transmission congestion. [e22] It says congestion lowers nodal prices in regions with high renewable capacity [e23], and that the West Zone is the largest contributor to intrazonal congestion costs: export constrained in the Panhandle, import constrained in the Permian Basin. [e24]
- **ERCOT context #3** (cites e25, e27, e26, e39, e28, e29, e31, e33): ERCOT's own instruments for price differences between two Settlement Points are CRRs and PTP Obligations. A CRR has a source Settlement Point and a different sink Settlement Point (Flowgate Rights excepted) and is purely financial. [e25][e27] A PTP Obligation settled in the DAM pays or charges the Day-Ahead price difference between sink and source (a positive-value one that sinks at a Resource Node may be paid less if network elements were oversold in earlier CRR Auctions); one bought in the DAM is settled on the Real-Time difference. [e26][e39][e28] ERCOT's training material presents a CRR as a hedge that locks in the cost of congestion at the CRR's purchase price, and the FERC primer describes the equivalent FTRs elsewhere as protection against congestion-driven day-ahead price increases. [e29][e31] The IMM says the financial right to congestion rent accrues to CRR holders. [e33]
- **ERCOT context #4** (cites e30): Gas basis matters to ERCOT generators too: the IMM says Permian production and limited export capability have historically kept Waha gas prices low, giving gas units in that area much higher net revenues. [e30]
- **Key facts #1** (cites e2, e8, e13): - Basis risk is also called locational (basis) risk or node-to-hub basis risk. [e2][e8][e13]
- **Key facts #2** (cites e2, e14): - Virtual PPAs are typically or frequently settled at a hub while the generator is paid at its node. [e2][e14]
- **Key facts #3** (cites e9, e13, e38): - Under hub settlement the project carries the node-to-hub basis risk. [e9][e13][e38]
- **Key facts #4** (cites e9, e10, e36): - Whoever carries the risk loses when the node price falls relative to the other price: the project against the hub (Norton Rose Fulbright), the buyer against its retail price (Yale). [e9][e10][e36]
- **Key facts #5** (cites e19): - ERCOT Settlement Points are Resource Nodes, Load Zones and Hubs. [e19]
- **Key facts #6** (cites e20): - ERCOT generators settle at nodal LMPs; loads at the load-weighted load zone price. [e20]
- **Key facts #7** (cites e26, e28): - A DAM PTP Obligation settles on the sink-minus-source price difference. [e26][e28]
- **Formula #1** (cites e1, e2, e6): Basis as a price is the difference between the prices at two locations. The sources state it as hub price minus nodal price for a hub-settled power contract [e1][e2], and as the difference between the Henry Hub price and another hub's price in gas. [e6]
- **Common confusion #2** (cites e2, e7, e3, e4, e1) FLAG number-not-in-quote: 2019: The sources do not all define it the same way. Norton Rose Fulbright and POWER Magazine frame it from the project's side, as node versus the hub where the contract settles. [e2][e7] RMI and Yale frame it from the buyer's side, as the generator's node versus the buyer's own load zone or retail price, and Yale describes it as a matter of correlation. [e3][e4] Norton Rose Fulbright's 2019 article even calls the price difference itself "basis risk". [e1] Read which two prices a speaker means.
- **Common confusion #3** (cites e13, e16): Settling at the hub reduces the buyer's basis risk but not the project's: Yale says the risk is shifted, not removed. [e13][e16]
- **Common confusion #4** (cites e6, e22): Gas basis and power basis are different spreads. Gas basis is a hub-to-hub differential driven by pipeline transport; power basis in ERCOT comes from transmission congestion between Settlement Points. [e6][e22]
- **Questions #2** (cites e13, e14, e35, e9): Hubs aggregate many nodes, so hub prices are less volatile than a single node's and hub settlement is seen as lower risk. [e13][e14] Norton Rose Fulbright adds that corporate offtakers and lenders prefer liquid, high-volume hubs, and that this may ease the analysis of price trends. [e35] The cost is that the node-to-hub basis risk falls on the project. [e9][e13]
- **Questions #4** (cites e11, e12, e23, e24): Clustered wind or solar congests the export lines in high-output hours and drives node prices down. [e11][e12] The IMM says congestion lowers nodal prices in high-renewable regions and that the West Zone, export constrained in the Panhandle, is the largest contributor to intrazonal congestion costs. [e23][e24]

- **Questions #8** (cites e34, e10): Norton Rose Fulbright says that under an as-produced VPPA it generally owes nothing for hours it does not produce, and that the loss arises when it does produce and the hub spikes more than its node, since it must cover the difference. [e34][e10]
- **Questions #10** (cites e26, e27, e28): A CRR or PTP Obligation runs from a source Settlement Point to a different sink Settlement Point and pays or charges the sink-minus-source price difference, so a position from the project's node to the hub moves in the direction of the basis loss. ## Uncited guidance text (check for new facts, numbers, dates, names)

- (Example) Suppose (illustrative, made-up numbers) a 100 MW West Texas wind project has a hub-settled virtual PPA. In one hour it produces 100 MWh, the hub price is $60/MWh and its node price is $40/MWh because the area is congested. The project earns 100 × $40 = $4,000 selling at its node but is settled against 100 × $60 = $6,000 at the hub, so basis costs it 100 × ($60 − $40) = $2,000 for that hour. If instead the node were $65 and the hub $60, basis would add 100 × $5 = $500.
- (Say it like this) (Guidance) Basis risk is the gap between where you get paid and where your hedge settles. In ERCOT a renewable project is paid at its own node, so a hub-settled contract leaves it exposed whenever congestion pushes its node below the hub.
- (Common confusion) (Guidance) Basis risk is general industry usage and is not defined in the ERCOT Protocols, PUCT rules or IMM reports saved here.
