- **Definition #1** (cites e1, e17, e18): For ERCOT market rules the reference natural gas price is the Fuel Index Price (FIP): the daily midpoint or average price of natural gas at the Katy Hub, in $/MMBtu. [e1] The national benchmark is Henry Hub, a pipeline hub on the Louisiana Gulf coast that is the delivery point for the NYMEX natural gas futures contract. [e17] EIA says most gas at U.S. trading hubs is priced relative to Henry Hub. [e18]
- **How it works #1** (cites e2, e29, e27, e19): Gas units are usually the marginal generators in ERCOT, so real-time power prices are driven mainly by gas prices and by shortage pricing. [e2] The IMM expects gas and energy prices to correlate in a competitive market because suppliers offer at marginal cost. [e29] ERCOT's 2024 ORDC report likewise attributed lower 2024 hub prices partly to lower gas prices, which it called highly correlated with spot power prices. [e27] EIA makes the same general point: price moves at gas hubs tend to show up at nearby power hubs. [e19]
- **How it works #2** (cites e4, e10, e5, e6, e7): That link is not one-for-one. In 2024 gas prices fell 14% but energy prices without adders fell almost 52%, which the IMM attributes to less artificial shortage pricing from ECRS deployment. [e4] In 2023 energy prices stayed about level with 2022 despite a 62% drop in gas prices, which the IMM calls a distortion from ECRS practices. [e10] In 2025 the gas price rose 61% while the all-in price of electricity rose less than 14%. [e5] The IMM gives two reasons: surplus supply meant expensive gas peakers set price less often, and high operating reserves meant little shortage pricing. [e6] More broadly, growing zero marginal cost wind and solar produces hundreds of hours at or below $0, weakening the gas-power link. [e7]
