## Uncited guidance text (check for new facts, numbers, dates, names)

- (Example) (Illustrative) Suppose the FIP is $3.00/MMBtu and a gas unit's heat rate is 10 MMBtu/MWh: its fuel cost is about $30/MWh. If the FIP rises 61%, to about $4.83, the same unit's fuel cost rises to about $48/MWh. Whether the power price follows depends on how often that unit is marginal and how much shortage pricing occurs, which is what happened in 2025 when power rose far less than gas.
- (Example) (Illustrative) Suppose the FIP is $3.00, a unit's fuel adder is $0.50 and it buys intraday gas at $4.20. The Exceptional Fuel Cost threshold is 3.00 + 1.00 + 0.50 = $4.50, so $4.20 would not qualify; at $5.00 it would clear the price threshold, though the other listed conditions still apply.
- (Say it like this) (Guidance) Gas plants are typically the marginal generator in ERCOT, so gas prices are a main driver of power prices. But with lots of wind and solar, the IMM says the link has weakened, so a gas move does not translate one-for-one into power.
- (Say it like this) (Guidance) When someone says "gas price" in ERCOT, ask which one: Katy (the FIP in the rules), Henry Hub (the national futures benchmark, used for FFSS) or Waha (West Texas, often far below the others).
