- **Definition #1** (cites e1, e2, e3, e44): A forward curve is the set of prices at which a commodity can be bought or sold today for delivery in each future period: each monthly contract that trades has a price, and together those prices form the forward (or futures) curve. [e1][e2][e3] Pirrong's lecture slides put it the same way: a forward curve relates the forward price to its delivery date. [e44]
- **How it works #1** (cites e1, e4, e43, e5, e6, e35, e63, e7): Standardized forwards, futures and swaps trade for each month, years into the future, and the curve is read from their prices. [e1] A forward contract is an agreement to receive or deliver a commodity at a specified future time; the CFTC adds that its terms can be more personalized than a standardized futures contract. It can be settled physically, or financially (a swap), in which case the parties exchange the gain or loss in cash. [e4][e43][e5] Futures are standardized forwards traded on an exchange, and FERC says they are generally more liquid than forwards; RFF likewise describes the monthly power futures traded at hubs in ERCOT and other U.S. markets as similar in design to forwards but with standardized terms and exchange trading. [e6][e35][e63] For bilateral power forwards, the EEI Master Power Purchase and Sale Agreement is commonly used, with price, quantity, location and delivery period set in a confirmation. [e7]
- **How it works #2** (cites e8, e33, e28, e29): Forward prices are updated every trading day: the SFU thesis says a new forward curve is created each day that power and gas trade, with distant months moving closer to the spot market over time, and FERC says daily settlement prices are used to revalue positions to current market prices. [e8][e33] Positions are marked to market: revalued each day at current prices, comparing the market price of undelivered volumes with the trade price. [e28][e29][e33]
- **How it works #3** (cites e1, e11, e12, e52, e61, e62) FLAG number-not-in-quote: 2024: Liquidity is concentrated at the front. FERC notes that NYMEX gas futures trade more than eight years out but only the first few years trade actively; the SFU thesis says more volume trades in near months and weeks, giving better price discovery there; the Fields slides note that 5-7 year tolls lie well outside the window in which individual months trade. [e1][e11][e12] Montel says liquidity can be lower for long-term contracts, making them harder to hedge. [e52] RFF, citing a 2017 study, puts a number on it for U.S. power: electricity forwards trade liquidly only one to two years out, while Henry Hub gas contracts trade out 10 to 12 years. FERC's 2024 primer says NYMEX gas trades more than eight years out, with only the first few years active. [e61][e62][e1]
- **How it works #4** (cites e13, e3, e2, e46, e47): The sources do not agree on how far forward prices are forecasts. The New Zealand regulator calls them the consensus view of future prices, reflecting expected demand and generation costs. [e13][e3] The SFU thesis says forward prices are based on predicted spot prices but adjusted for risk tolerance and net expense. [e2] Pirrong's slides go further: a forward price is not a price forecast or expectation, it embeds a risk adjustment, and in power that adjustment is very large. Among the possible sources of that premium his slides list a "Keynes-type story": power prices can spike, imposing big losses on sellers (shorts), who demand a big premium to sell forward. [e46][e47] The common ground is that forward prices start from expected spot conditions; SFU and Pirrong add a risk adjustment that the New Zealand article does not mention. [e2][e46][e13]
- **How it works #5** (cites e14, e51, e30): Expected conditions in the delivery period shape the curve. Two publishers say forward prices carry a premium for expected high-demand periods: the Fields slides attribute the seasonality of a PJM forward curve (higher in summer and, to a lesser extent, winter) to expected higher loads pushing clearing prices up the supply stack, and Montel, writing about European markets, says seasonality produces premiums in high-demand periods such as winter. [e14][e51] The New Zealand regulator lists seasonal climate expectations (El Niño or La Niña) as a factor. [e30]
- **How it works #6** (cites e39, e40, e45, e49, e64, e41, e50): The overall slope has names. The CFTC defines contango as prices in succeeding delivery months progressively higher than the nearest month, and backwardation as futures prices progressively lower in distant months; Pirrong, Montel and the SFU thesis describe the same upward and downward curves. [e39][e40][e45][e49][e64] The sources compare against slightly different points: the CFTC against the nearest delivery month, Montel against the spot or current price. [e39][e49] On causes, the CFTC calls an inverted (backwardated) market characteristic of supply shortages and Montel says backwardation is usually due to supply issues pushing up the current price. [e41][e50] SFU says the slope depends partly on convenience yield, expectations about long-term fundamentals and the risk-free rate, and Pirrong that contango need not cover carrying charges. [e64][e45]
- **How it works #7** (cites e9, e10, e31, e38): Two sources agree that the near end of the curve is the more volatile: the SFU thesis says volatility is extremely high in the real-time, next-day and balance-of-week portions and falls for distant months, and the New Zealand regulator says hydro storage makes short-dated prices potentially more volatile than long-dated ones (a hydro-specific reason). - (Example) Suppose (illustrative, made-up numbers) North Hub peak forwards are $45 for May, $95 for August and $50 for November. A retailer that buys 10 MW of August peak at $95 has fixed that month's price. If the August price later marks at $110, the position gains $15/MWh on paper before delivery; if real-time August peak prices average $80, the hedge settles at a loss against spot but the retailer paid the price it planned for.
- (Say it like this) (Guidance) "The forward curve is today's price for each future delivery month: the market's tradable view, not a forecast you can ignore. Liquidity is at the front; far-dated months trade thinly, so ask whether a price is a trade or a mark."
