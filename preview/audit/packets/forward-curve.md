## Statements to check (new or changed since the last audit)

- **Definition #1** (cites e1, e2, e3): A forward curve is the set of prices at which a commodity can be bought or sold today for delivery in each future period: each monthly contract that trades has a price, and together those prices form the forward (or futures) curve. [e1][e2][e3]
- **How it works #1** (cites e1, e4, e5, e6, e35, e7): Standardized forwards, futures and swaps trade for each month, years into the future, and the curve is read from their prices. [e1] A forward contract is an agreement to receive or deliver a commodity at a specified future time; it can be settled physically, or financially (a swap), in which case the parties exchange the gain or loss in cash. [e4][e5] Futures are standardized forwards traded on an exchange and are generally more liquid than forwards. [e6][e35] For bilateral power forwards, the EEI Master Power Purchase and Sale Agreement is commonly used, with price, quantity, location and delivery period set in a confirmation. [e7]
- **How it works #2** (cites e8, e33, e28, e29): Forward prices are updated every trading day: the SFU thesis says a new forward curve is created each day that power and gas trade, with distant months moving closer to the spot market over time, and FERC says daily settlement prices are used to revalue positions to current market prices. [e8][e33] Positions are marked to market: revalued each day at current prices, comparing the market price of undelivered volumes with the trade price. [e28][e29][e33]
- **How it works #3** (cites e1, e11, e12): Liquidity is concentrated at the front. FERC notes that NYMEX gas futures trade more than eight years out but only the first few years trade actively; the SFU thesis says more volume trades in near months and weeks, giving better price discovery there; the Fields slides note that 5-7 year tolls lie well outside the window in which individual months trade. [e1][e11][e12]
- **How it works #4** (cites e13, e3, e2, e14, e30): The sources say forward prices reflect what the market expects spot prices to be. The New Zealand regulator calls them the consensus view of future prices, reflecting expected demand and generation costs; the SFU thesis says forward prices are based on predicted spot prices adjusted for risk tolerance and net expense. [e13][e3][e2] Two other sources name expected conditions in the delivery period as a driver: the Fields slides attribute the seasonality of a PJM forward curve (higher in summer and, to a lesser extent, winter) to expected higher loads pushing clearing prices up the supply stack, and the New Zealand regulator lists seasonal climate expectations (El Niño or La Niña) as a factor. [e14][e30]
- **How it works #5** (cites e9, e10, e31, e38): Two sources agree that the near end of the curve is the more volatile: the SFU thesis says volatility is extremely high in the real-time, next-day and balance-of-week portions and falls for distant months, and the New Zealand regulator says hydro storage makes short-dated prices potentially more volatile than long-dated ones (a hydro-specific reason). [e9][e10] A vendor blog on European markets (Montel) links volatility to the lower liquidity of the forward market, but the same blog also says day-ahead is more volatile than the forward market, so it does not establish a disagreement. [e31][e38]
- **How it works #6** (cites e15, e16, e27, e24): Power curves are quoted by delivery bucket as well as by month. Fixed-price power swaps settle on the monthly average spot price for a prescribed bucket, and the ICE ERCOT North peak future settles on the monthly average of peak hourly prices. [e15][e16] Power also trades in heat-rate terms: the Fields slides say power swaps often trade as heat rates, a power trade paired with an offsetting gas volume over the same period, and ICE lists an ERCOT heat-rate contract whose final settlement is the traded heat rate times the NYMEX last-day gas settlement price. [e27][e24]
- **ERCOT context #1** (cites e21, e22, e19, e17, e34): ERCOT's own training calls the Day-Ahead Market a centralized forward market, conducted daily and facilitated by ERCOT, and bilateral trades a decentralized forward market of QSE-to-QSE transactions that transfer settlement responsibility. [e21][e22] The IMM also refers to bilateral forward markets in ERCOT, and on exchanges ICE lists the ERCOT North 345KV real-time peak future for up to 50 consecutive monthly contract periods, and an ERCOT Panhandle heat-rate contract for up to 72 monthly periods. [e19][e17][e34]
- **ERCOT context #2** (cites e18, e16): The ICE ERCOT North peak contract covers Monday through Friday, excluding NERC holidays, for hours ending 0700-2200 CPT, and settles on the monthly average of ERCOT-published real-time prices at that hub. [e18][e16]
- **ERCOT context #3** (cites e19, e20, e32): The IMM says only a small share of ERCOT power is settled in real time, but real-time prices shape expectations in the day-ahead and bilateral forward markets and are the principal driver of prices where most transactions occur. [e19] It treats convergence of forward (day-ahead) prices with real-time prices as a primary indicator of forward market performance. [e20] Because forward prices are highly correlated with spot prices, the IMM notes that raising real-time prices can also raise a supplier's profits in the bilateral market. [e32]

- (Example) Suppose (illustrative, made-up numbers) North Hub peak forwards are $45 for May, $95 for August and $50 for November. A retailer that buys 10 MW of August peak at $95 has fixed that month's price. If the August price later marks at $110, the position gains $15/MWh on paper before delivery; if real-time August peak prices average $80, the hedge settles at a loss against spot but the retailer paid the price it planned for.
- (Say it like this) (Guidance) "The forward curve is today's price for each future delivery month: the market's tradable view, not a forecast you can ignore. Liquidity is at the front; far-dated months trade thinly, so ask whether a price is a trade or a mark."
- (Common confusion) (Guidance) Forward curve is general industry usage; it is not defined in the ERCOT Protocols, PUCT rules or IMM reports saved here.
