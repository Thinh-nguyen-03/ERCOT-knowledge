## Statements to check

- **Definition #1** (cites e1, e4, e12): A shadow price is the marginal value of a commodity: the rate at which system costs would fall or rise if slightly more or less of it were made available. [e1] For a transmission constraint, the IMM describes it as the marginal cost of redirecting the flow of energy around a binding constraint. [e4] In ERCOT's methodology document, a transmission constraint's shadow price is a positive $/MW amount defined as the increase in system dispatch cost if the line limit is decreased by one MW. [e12]
- **How it works #1** (cites e3, e31, e2, e9): SCED's second step produces Base Points, Ancillary Service awards, Shadow Prices, Real-Time MCPCs and LMPs, subject to the security constraints. [e3] The SCED objective function has four components: the cost of dispatching generation, the cost of procuring Ancillary Services, the penalty for violating the power balance constraint, and the penalty for violating network transmission constraints. [e31] ERCOT sets a maximum shadow price for each network constraint as part of its contingency definition, and the cost SCED calculates to resolve another MW of congestion on that constraint cannot exceed it. A separate maximum applies to the power balance constraint. [e2][e9]
- **How it works #2** (cites e13, e8, e7): If a constraint is not binding, its shadow price is $0.00/MWh. If it is binding, the shadow price is a non-zero $/MW value, and once the cap is reached the constraint is violated without further increases in the shadow price. [e13] Per the IMM, violations impose reliability costs or risks on the system, which ERCOT embeds in the shadow price caps. When the marginal cost of relief through dispatch exceeds the reliability cost of violating the constraint, the cap stops further dispatch cost and sets the shadow price, which determines congestion prices at the locations that affect the violated constraint. [e8] The IMM calculates day-ahead congestion values as the product of power flows over each constraint and the constraint's shadow price. [e7]
- **How it works #3** (cites e11, e10): **Who sets the caps.** Section 6.5.7.1.11 requires PUCT approval of ERCOT's methodology and of the values of the caps, per Attachment P. [e11] The caps directly determine real-time LMPs in cases of constraint violations. [e10]
- **How it works #4** (cites e18, e19, e38): **Generic caps.** Unless ERCOT determines a constraint is irresolvable by SCED, the generic caps apply: $5,251/MW for a base case or voltage violation, and for N-1 constraint violations $4,500/MW above 200 kV, $3,500/MW from 100 to 200 kV and $2,800/MW below 100 kV (Attachment P, December 5, 2025 version). [e18][e19] The IMM lists the 2025 caps as $5,251 per MW for base-case or voltage constraints, $4,500 for 345 kV, $3,500 for 138 kV and $2,800 for 69 kV thermal violations, and says GTCs are treated as base-case stability constraints at $5,251. [e38]
- **How it works #5** (cites e20, e21, e22): **Why those levels.** ERCOT's supporting analysis shows how a cap maps to which offers will be used: with a 2% shift factor difference between marginal units, the maximum offer price difference of units deployed to resolve a constraint is $56, $70, $90 and $105.02/MWh for caps of $2,800, $3,500, $4,500 and $5,251/MW. [e20] It also argues that a cap high enough to always accept an offer at the RTSWCAP is not workable: at a 2% shift factor difference, a $100,000/MW cap would be required to deploy $2,000/MWh offers. [e21] ERCOT notes that an LMP at a node, hub or load zone can exceed the RTSWCAP in some circumstances, most likely with irresolvable constraints and tight dispatchable supply. [e22]
- **How it works #6** (cites e24, e25, e26, e27, e29, e28): **Irresolvable constraints.** ERCOT modifies the cap for a constraint consistently irresolvable by SCED if a violation goes unresolved for more than two consecutive hours on more than 4 consecutive Operating Days, or for more than a total of 20 hours in a rolling thirty-day period. [e24] The cap becomes the minimum of the generic cap and the greater of a reference Resource's largest Mitigated Offer Cap divided by its shift factor, or $2000 per MW. [e25] If the constraint's accumulated net margin exceeds $95,000/MW, the cap is lowered for the rest of the calendar year to the minimum of $2,000/MWh or a second formula. [e26] Net margin is a running sum of one quarter times the greater of zero or the reference Settlement Point Price minus a Proxy Operating Cost, over intervals in which the constraint binds; the Proxy Operating Cost is 10 times the Fuel Index Price for the prior Business Day. [e27] ERCOT gives 30 days notice before deeming a constraint resolvable again, and the IMM may initiate re-evaluation, which resets the net margin. [e29][e28]
- **How it works #7** (cites e23): **After the cap is reached.** ERCOT may form a mitigation plan (switching, load rollover, load shed plans, redistribution of Ancillary Services), commit additional units, or override HDLs and LDLs. [e23]
- **How it works #8** (cites e30): **IROLs.** An IROL's cap is set to the generic base-case cap, or, if ERCOT in its sole discretion finds that insufficient, to the power balance penalty price minus the mitigated offer floor of a reference Resource divided by its shift factor impact. [e30]
- **How it works #9** (cites e12, e10, e32) FLAG number-not-in-quote: 2025: **Power balance cap.** For the power balance constraint, the shadow price is System Lambda and the cap is the Power Balance Penalty. [e12][e10] In the December 5, 2025 document the under-generation penalty is a single value equal to the effective VOLL plus the effective RTSWCAP plus $4,052.01/MWh, and the over-generation penalty is -$250/MWh. [e32]
- **Formula #1** (cites e14, e15, e16, e17): The Attachment P LMP at an Electrical Bus is System Lambda minus the sum, over constraints, of the bus's shift factor times the constraint's shadow price. [e14] Variables: LMP_EB is the LMP at electrical bus EB; lambda is System Lambda, the shadow price of power balance; SF is the shift factor of that bus for the line; SP is the line's shadow price. [e14] Shadow prices of congested lines are positive and otherwise zero, and shift factors are negative on one side of a line and positive on the other. [e15] The congestion component of the bus LMP is the negative of the sum of shift factor times shadow price, so it can be positive or negative; it is the price incentive for units at that bus to raise or lower output to manage congestion. [e16] Attachment P also notes that generating units with small shift factors are less effective at resolving constraints, so if no efficient units exist the shadow price must rise, which it says means high shadow prices indicate inefficient congestion management. [e17]
- **Formula #2** (cites e16) FLAG number-not-in-quote: $2,000, 0.5, $1,000: Worked illustration (Suppose, not a quote): with a $2,000/MW shadow price on one constraint and no other constraints, a bus with shift factor -0.5 gets a congestion component of +$1,000/MWh under the formula above. [e16]
- **ERCOT context #1** (cites e39): The IMM says most 2025 violations were 5% or less above the limit yet are priced at the same cap as severe ones, and suggests reconsidering transmission demand curves so small violations get lower shadow prices; this is the IMM's recommendation, not a rule. [e39]
- **ERCOT context #2** (cites e5): The IMM reports that ERCOT implemented NPRR 1230 at the end of 2024 to raise the cap on base-case constraints, which lets SCED choose more expensive dispatch to avoid violations. [e5]
- **ERCOT context #3** (cites e35, e36, e37): In the day-ahead market the transmission constraints are enforced with far larger penalty values: the Protocols require transmission limits to be satisfied in the DAM, so the penalty values are set very high, for example 1,050,000 for a base case 150+ kV constraint and 1,000,000 for a 150+ kV contingency constraint and for non-thermal (generic) constraints in Table 1-1. [e35][e36] ERCOT says DAM penalties are much higher than the SCED caps because DAM is a unit commitment problem needing to reflect start-up and minimum-energy costs, whereas SCED penalties need only be on the order of energy offer cost. [e37]

[e33][e34]

- **Timeline #3** (cites e32, e19) FLAG number-not-in-quote: 2025: - December 5, 2025 version: power balance penalty is VOLL plus RTSWCAP plus $4,052.01/MWh (over-generation -$250/MWh); generic caps $5,251, $4,500, $3,500, $2,800 per MW. [e32][e19]
- **Key facts #1** (cites e2, e9): - ERCOT sets a maximum shadow price per network constraint and for the power balance constraint. [e2][e9]
- **Key facts #2** (cites e2): - SCED's cost to resolve one more MW of congestion is limited to that maximum. [e2]
- **Key facts #3** (cites e19) FLAG number-not-in-quote: 2025: - Generic caps (Dec. 5, 2025): $5,251/MW base case or voltage; $4,500, $3,500, $2,800 by voltage class for N-1. [e19]

- **Key facts #5** (cites e26): - Net margin threshold that lowers the cap for the calendar year: $95,000/MW. [e26]
- **Key facts #6** (cites e13): - A non-binding constraint has a $0.00/MWh shadow price. [e13]
- **Key facts #7** (cites e5): - NPRR 1230, implemented at the end of 2024, raised the cap on base-case constraints. [e5]
- **Say it like this #1** (cites e8): A shadow price is what it costs the system, at the margin, to squeeze one more MW through a binding constraint. ERCOT caps it at a reliability-based value, so when relief would cost more than the reliability cost of a violation, the cap sets the shadow price. [e8]
- **Common confusion #1** (cites e7, e3, e38, e19): The shadow price is a marginal cost per MW, not a total congestion cost; the IMM's day-ahead congestion values multiply it by flow over each constraint. [e7] It is also not a bid or offer: it comes out of the SCED solution. [e3] The IMM's voltage labels (345, 138 and 69 kV) and Attachment P's labels (above 200 kV, 100 to 200 kV, below 100 kV) describe the same three dollar levels in different words. [e38][e19]
- **Questions #2** (cites e8, e2, e21): When relief through dispatch would cost more than the reliability cost of a violation, the cap limits the dispatch cost and sets the shadow price rather than letting it rise further. [e8][e2] ERCOT also argues no cap can always accept an RTSWCAP offer without producing congestion prices far above reasonable scarcity levels. [e21]
- **Questions #4** (cites e5): It raised the cap on base-case constraints so SCED can find more expensive dispatch solutions to avoid violating them. [e5]
- **Questions #6** (cites e14, e7): The bus LMP is System Lambda minus the sum of shift factor times shadow price, and the IMM values day-ahead congestion as flow times shadow price. [e14][e7]
- **Questions #8** (cites e21, e22) FLAG number-not-in-quote: 50%, $50,000: Because congestion components add to System Lambda: with a -50% shift factor and a $100,000/MW cap, one constraint alone would give a $50,000/MWh component; ERCOT says LMPs can exceed the cap when constraints are irresolvable and supply is tight. [e21][e22]
- **Questions #10** (cites e24, e25, e26): If unresolved more than 2 consecutive hours on more than 4 consecutive days, or 20 hours in 30 days, ERCOT resets its cap using a reference Resource's mitigated offer cap, and may lower it further after $95,000/MW of net margin. [e24][e25][e26]
- **Questions #12** (cites e37): DAM is a unit commitment problem, so penalties reflect start-up and minimum-energy costs; SCED is an economic dispatch problem, so caps only need to be on the order of energy offer cost. [e37]
- **Questions #14** (cites e12, e10): System Lambda: the cost of one more MWh at the reference bus, capped by the Power Balance Penalty. [e12][e10]
