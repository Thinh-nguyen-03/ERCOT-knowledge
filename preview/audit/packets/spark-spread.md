## Statements to check (new or changed since the last audit)

- **Definition #1** (cites e30, e1, e2, e32): The CFTC glossary defines the spark spread as the differential between the price of electricity and the price of natural gas or other fuel used to generate it, expressed in equivalent units. [e30] EIA and the industry sources say the same in plant terms: the difference between the price a generator receives for its electricity and the cost of the natural gas needed to produce it, used as a measure of the profitability of running a gas-fired plant. [e1][e2][e32]
- **How it works #1** (cites e3, e4, e31, e32, e8): The fuel cost is the gas price multiplied by a heat rate, so the spark spread is the power price minus gas price times heat rate, in $/MWh. EIA, the SFU thesis and Deng et al. all state it this way. [e3][e4][e31] The result is the margin a plant would earn if it bought gas, converted it to power at the stated heat rate and sold the power at the market price; Penn State describes the same buy-fuel, sell-power opportunity. [e4][e32] The heat rate is the plant's conversion rate between gas and power. [e8][e3]
- **How it works #2** (cites e5, e37, e32, e31, e20, e4): Which heat rate is used depends on the purpose, and the sources assume different ones. Someone marketing one unit uses that unit's tested heat rate; observers of the whole market use a generic benchmark, and EIA uses 7,000 Btu/kWh, representing a fairly new and efficient combined-cycle unit. [e5][e37] Penn State's course text says spark spreads in financial markets are typically defined for a gas generator with a heat rate of 10,000 and variable O&M of $2.50 per MWh. [e32] Deng et al. define the spread for "a particular heat rate" without fixing one. [e31] A less efficient unit (higher heat rate) needs more fuel per MWh and so earns a smaller spark spread. [e20][e4][e31]
- **How it works #3** (cites e7, e32, e33, e2, e6, e8): The sources treat other costs differently, and you should say which version you mean. EIA's spark spread leaves out other costs such as pipeline charges, variable O&M, taxes and fixed costs, so it is an indicator of market conditions rather than an exact profit figure for one plant. [e7] Penn State's definition, by contrast, subtracts variable O&M along with fuel. [e32][e33] The SFU thesis says a spark spread may or may not include fixed or variable O&M depending on context, and separates a gross spread from a net spread that also deducts at least gas transportation and power transmission costs. [e2][e6] The Fields Institute toll formula subtracts VOM (variable operation and maintenance) as well as heat rate times gas. [e8]
- **How it works #4** (cites e1, e9, e36, e32, e8, e31, e34, e28) FLAG number-not-in-quote: 2013, 2017: The sources also differ on which power price goes in. EIA's 2013 spark spread article says it is typically calculated from daily spot prices, its 2017 article uses day-ahead wholesale prices, and its 2013 dark spread article says spark spreads use on-peak power prices. [e1][e9][e36] Penn State uses the LMP, the Fields slides the forward or spot price for a delivery bucket such as 5x16, and Deng et al. the current price. [e32][e8][e31] Both Penn State and EIA's market reporting treat a negative spread as possible: Penn State's PJM example has negative and positive hours, and EIA's 2013 chart simply leaves negative values out. [e34][e28]
- **How it works #6** (cites e11, e35, e36, e37, e38, e39, e12, e9): The same idea is applied to other fuels: the dark spread for coal and the quark spread for nuclear fuel. [e11] EIA defines the dark spread as the difference between the price a generator receives for electricity and the cost of the coal needed to produce it, calculated on spot or forward prices. [e35] It differs from the spark spread in its inputs: EIA's generic dark spread uses a flat (on- plus off-peak) power price rather than on-peak, a 10,000 Btu/kWh coal heat rate rather than 7,000, and adds a transportation cost to the coal futures price. [e36][e37][e38] EIA says the generic dark spread has the same limitation as the spark spread: it leaves out finance charges, variable O&M and taxes, and environmental costs unless they are added. [e39] Because fuels and plant efficiencies differ, spark and dark spreads are specific to a location and a market. [e12][e9]
- **Formula #1** (cites e3, e4, e31): Spark spread ($/MWh) = power price ($/MWh) - [natural gas price ($/MMBtu) x heat rate (MMBtu/MWh)]. [e3][e4][e31]
- **Formula #2** (cites e30, e3): - Units: the CFTC says the two prices are expressed in equivalent units; the heat rate is what converts $/MMBtu of gas into $/MWh of power. [e30][e3]
- **Formula #3** (cites e1, e36, e9, e32, e8) FLAG number-not-in-quote: 2017: - Power price: the price received for electricity in the same period. EIA typically uses daily spot prices at regional trading points (on-peak, per its dark spread article), its 2017 article day-ahead prices; Penn State uses the LMP; Fields a forward or spot price for a delivery bucket. [e1][e36][e9][e32][e8]
- **Formula #5** (cites e8, e5, e37, e32): - Heat rate: the plant's conversion rate between gas and power, in MMBtu per MWh (7,000 Btu/kWh is 7.0 MMBtu/MWh). EIA assumes 7,000 Btu/kWh for a benchmark combined cycle; Penn State's typical financial-market definition assumes 10,000; for one unit, its tested heat rate is used. [e8][e5][e37][e32]
- **Formula #6** (cites e7, e32, e33, e8, e6): - Other costs: EIA's version subtracts nothing else. [e7] Penn State subtracts variable O&M ($2.50/MWh in its example), Fields subtracts VOM, and SFU's net version subtracts at least gas transportation and power transmission costs. [e32][e33][e8][e6]
- **Key facts #2** (cites e27, e37) FLAG number-not-in-quote: 2025, 2013: - EIA's December 2025 article used 7,000 Btu/kWh for the spark spread and 10,500 Btu/kWh for the dark spread; its February 2013 dark spread article used a generic 10,000 Btu/kWh coal unit, slightly above 30% efficient.
