## Statements to check

- **Definition #1** (cites e1, e2, e3, e7): Under the Protocols, ERCOT produces and uses Load forecasts to serve operations and planning objectives. [e1] The main products are an hourly Seven-Day Load Forecast by Weather Zone and Forecast Zone, a monthly 36-Month Load Forecast, and a rolling two-hour, five-minute Intra-Hour Load Forecast. [e2][e3][e7]
- **How it works #1** (cites e4, e5, e3): The Seven-Day Load Forecast predicts hourly Loads for the next 168 hours from current weather forecast parameters in each Weather Zone, because Day-Ahead operations require an accurate forecast of the Loads for which capacity must be secured. [e4] ERCOT reviews the result and may modify it with judgment before it is used in the Ancillary Service Capacity Monitor, DRUC, HRUC and resource adequacy reporting. [e5] The 36-Month Load Forecast gives daily minimum and maximum Load and is used in outage coordination and resource adequacy reporting. [e3]
- **How it works #2** (cites e12, e8, e11, e15): For the long term, ERCOT prepares at least annually a forecast of hourly Loads for a minimum of ten years using an econometric forecast with weather conditions and demographic data. [e12] The 2025 forecast document describes econometric models of hourly load as a function of premises by customer class, weather variables and calendar variables, and a normal-weather summer peak formed by averaging the summer peaks from seventeen weather scenarios, which are built from actual weather for 2008 through 2024. [e8][e11][e15]
- **ERCOT context #1** (cites e10, e6, e17, e9): Forecast accuracy affects commitment. The IMM says an over-forecast of net demand can lead to sub-optimal commitment, and an under-forecast can leave real-time shortfalls that are often addressed through RUC. [e10] The IMM reports that the average net load forecast error (measured eight hours ahead of real-time) fell to about 452 MW in 2025 from more than 1,000 MW in 2023 and 2024, but forecasts still systematically over-forecast load and under-forecast renewable output. [e6][e17] The IMM calls the LTLF ERCOT's primary tool for projecting load growth over ten years. [e9]
- **Key facts #1** (cites e2, e4): - Seven-Day Load Forecast: hourly, 168 hours, by Weather Zone and Forecast Zone, posted hourly. [e2][e4]
- **Key facts #2** (cites e3): - 36-Month Load Forecast: monthly, daily min and max Load. [e3]
- **Key facts #3** (cites e7): - Intra-Hour Load Forecast: rolling two hours in five-minute increments, ERCOT-wide. [e7]
- **Key facts #4** (cites e6, e17): - 2025 average net load forecast error about 452 MW, measured eight hours ahead of real-time; forecasts continue to over-forecast load (IMM). [e6][e17]
- **Key facts #5** (cites e11, e15): - Long-term forecasts use seventeen weather-year scenarios (2008-2024) in the 2025 document. [e11][e15]
- **Say it like this #1** (cites e4, e5, e6, e10): (Guidance) ERCOT runs several load forecasts on different horizons, with weather as the main short-term driver, and ERCOT can use judgment to modify the Seven-Day result. [e4][e5] The practical risk is bias: the IMM says forecasts continue to over-forecast load, and that over-forecasting net demand can lead to sub-optimal commitment. [e6][e10]
- **Common confusion #1** (cites e16, e10, e12): (Guidance) Net Load is Load minus wind and PV generation [e16], and the IMM ties commitment risk to the net demand forecast rather than load alone. [e10] Also keep the short-term operational forecast separate from the long-term (LTLF) forecast used for planning. [e12]
