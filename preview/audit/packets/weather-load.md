## Statements to check

- **Definition #1** (cites e1): In the IMM's State of the Market reports, weather's effect on electricity use is measured by heating and cooling degree days, which are the amount by which average daily temperatures are above or below 65 °F. [e1] Residential and commercial demand rises quickly as cooling degree days grow, because of air conditioning. [e1]
- **How it works #1** (cites e15, e9, e7): In ERCOT's long-term forecasting, hourly load models use weather variables such as temperature values alongside premises and calendar variables. [e15] The forecast is built by applying weather data from each historical year (2008 through 2024) one at a time. [e9] The operational Seven-Day Load Forecast is driven by current weather forecast parameters for each Weather Zone. [e7]
- **How it works #2** (cites e3, e4): Weather moves peak demand year to year while the underlying trend keeps growing. In 2024 average demand rose about 3.5% but peak demand fell 0.3% because of less extreme summer heat. [e3] In 2025 average load rose 6.1% while peak load fell 1.8%. [e4]
- **ERCOT context #1** (cites e2, e11, e5, e6): In 2023 and 2026, hotter summers coincided with record peaks. Cooling degree days rose 8.5% in summer 2023, with numerous record load levels culminating on August 10 at a peak of 85.7 GW, which ERCOT's review attributes to economic growth and hotter temperatures. [e2][e11] ERCOT's summer 2026 review ranked June-July 2026 as the 11th hottest of 132 years and reported a new record of 91,134 MW on July 22, 2026. [e5][e6]
- **ERCOT context #2** (cites e8, e10) FLAG number-not-in-quote: 2021: Weather also acts on supply. The IMM notes that rising ambient temperatures generally cause thermal units' capability to fall. [e8] On the cold side, freezing issues caused 44.2% of the outages, derates and failures to start in the February 2021 event, according to the FERC/NERC report. [e10]
- **Key facts #1** (cites e1): - CDD/HDD are measured against a 65 °F base in the IMM reports. [e1]

[e3][e4] Also, hot weather raises gross load, but the the IMM says the tightest hours have moved to the later evening (7 to 9 p.m.), so a high-temperature day is not the same as a high-risk hour. ## Uncited guidance text (check for new facts, numbers, dates, names)

- (Say it like this) (Guidance) Load has two drivers: a steady structural climb from population, industry and large loads, and a weather overlay measured in degree days. Peak records need both a high base and heat, which is why a milder summer can show a lower peak even when average load is growing.
