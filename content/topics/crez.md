## Definition

Competitive Renewable Energy Zones (CREZ) were zones for which the Texas Legislature, via SB 408 in 2005, directed the PUCT to facilitate the design and construction of new transmission. [e1] SB 408 provided authority for the PUCT to direct ERCOT to plan for transmission to connect approximately 18 GW of wind capacity. [e3] The PUCT's rule, 16 TAC 25.174, sets out how zones were designated and how transmission for them was ordered and built. [e4][e7]

## How it works

The mechanism began legislatively: SB 408 designated the framework and authorized the PUCT to direct ERCOT to plan the transmission. [e3] It came alongside an increase in the renewable portfolio standard (RPS) to 5,880 MW of renewable capacity. [e1]

Under the PUCT rule, zones are designated through contested-case proceedings that commission staff initiate. [e4] ERCOT had to give the commission a study of statewide wind production potential and the transmission constraints most likely to limit deliverability. [e5] The commission issues a final order within six months of the proceeding's start, unless it finds good cause to extend, and each order specifies the geographic extent of the CREZ. [e6] Entities then express interest in building the transmission, and the commission selects who builds and sets a completion schedule. [e7]

Before CCNs are granted, generators must show financial commitment. It is sufficient if installed, under-construction, planned (with signed interconnection agreements) and collateral-backed capacity reaches at least 50% of the zone's designated capacity. [e8] The rule fixes 50% at 1,595.5 MW for Panhandle A and 1,196.5 MW for Panhandle B. [e9] Collateral is $15,350 per MW, or $10,000 per MW where supported by long-term land leases. [e10]

## ERCOT context

By the end of 2020, approximately 25,000 MW of wind and 4,000 MW of solar photovoltaic capacity were installed in ERCOT, surpassing the RPS. [e2] The rule anticipates that too much generation in a zone could cause congestion that SCED alone cannot resolve; in that case the commission may consider limiting interconnection or setting dispatch priorities in the CREZ. [e11]

## Key facts

- SB 408 (2005) created the CREZ framework and raised the RPS to 5,880 MW. [e1]
- Transmission planning was for roughly 18 GW of wind capacity. [e3]
- Zones are designated in PUCT contested cases, with a final order due within six months unless extended. [e4][e6]
- Financial commitment threshold: at least 50% of designated capacity (1,595.5 MW for Panhandle A, 1,196.5 MW for Panhandle B). [e8][e9]
- Collateral for planned projects: $15,350 per MW, or $10,000 per MW with 20-year land leases. [e10]
- End of 2020: about 25,000 MW of wind and 4,000 MW of solar PV installed in ERCOT. [e2]

## Say it like this

(Guidance) CREZ was the Texas policy of designating renewable zones and ordering new transmission to carry their output, planned around about 18 GW of wind. [e3] The PUCT's rule covers how zones were picked, who built the lines and how generators had to show commitment.

## Common confusion

CREZ is a transmission-planning policy, not a market product like a CRR; the saved sources do not describe CREZ as a settlement or pricing mechanism. [e1][e3] The rule does, however, contemplate congestion from excess development. [e11]

## Questions

### What was CREZ?

A 2005 legislative initiative (SB 408) directing the PUCT to facilitate new transmission to serve designated Competitive Renewable Energy Zones, with planning for about 18 GW of wind. [e1][e3]

### How did a zone get designated and built out?

Through a PUCT contested case, with a final order specifying the zone, then selection of transmission builders and a schedule. [e4][e6][e7]

### How did generators show commitment to a zone?

By reaching at least 50% of the zone's designated capacity through installed, under-construction, signed-interconnection or collateral-backed capacity. [e8]
